1 · Mental model A dead device is not broken. It is paused at a specific, findable point.

Every electronic fault is a location before it is a component. The device worked once, which means energy flowed along a defined path through defined stages — and now it stops somewhere. Your entire job is to bracket that stopping point between two measurements, walk the bracket inward, and condemn exactly one part with evidence. The five channels this guide is built from disagree about gear, era, and pace. They do not disagree about that.

Watch enough master-level repair and the surface differences dissolve. Mr. Carlson spends three hours on a 1938 tube radio; Alex at Northridge Fix spends thirteen minutes on an Asus laptop; Sorin at Electronics Repair School walks a Lenovo board with no schematic at all. Different universes of hardware — and underneath, the same loop:

  1. Observe. Read the ticket, then the board, before any probe touches it. Burn marks, corrosion, prior-repair damage, missing parts.
  2. Bracket. Find two points where behavior differs from expectation. The fault lives between them.
  3. Halve. Measure, inject, or disconnect at the midpoint of the bracket. One half is now exonerated.
  4. Condemn with proof. Out-of-circuit confirmation before the iron comes out. In-circuit readings lie.
  5. Repair, then verify like a skeptic. Real loads, real current draws, real signals — not "it powers on, ship it."

The three ideas that separate pros from parts-changers

First: measure what the meter actually sees. Sorin's most-repeated warning: low resistance at a VRM output is usually the CPU die itself, measured backwards through the coil — a coil is just wire. Most "I found a shorted MOSFET" emails he receives are this exact false positive. The meter reports the resistance of the entire path it sits across, not the part you are staring at. Know the path, or the number is noise.

Second: the schematic is optional, the topology is not. Carlson repairs supplies with no schematic by recognizing the converter topology — once you know it is a half-bridge, its failure modes follow. Sorin reads unknown boards by eye: every coil is a switching supply, every cap cluster marks a rail. Richard at Learn Electronics Repair rebuilt unobtainable circuits by sketching them from the board itself. The schematic is a convenience. Topology knowledge is the real instrument.

Third: replace only what testing proves bad. Carlson's rule, and it is the difference between repair and vandalism: wholesale recapping that happens to fix the fault teaches you nothing and destroys original design intent. Diagnose to the failed part. Then replace that part, correctly, and verify the repair with instruments.

What "grand unified" means here This guide merges five channels into one method because their methods already agree. Where they genuinely differ — thermal camera versus coil-shorting, donor-board pragmatism versus restore-to-factory discipline — this guide shows the fork and names who takes which branch and why. Section 4 is the merged spine; the application modules (§10–§12) show the spine running on real hardware from the survey.

2 · Prerequisite floor What this guide assumes you already bring to the bench

This guide starts at advanced-hobbyist, not zero. It assumes you have killed components before and learned from it. Below is the floor — the hard prerequisites you cannot proceed without, the soft ones that help, and a self-assessment to expose gaps while they are still cheap.

Hard prerequisites

  • DMM fluency: diode mode, resistance, DC volts, continuity — and you know why diode mode reads voltage drop, not ohms.
  • Component literacy: resistors, ceramic and electrolytic capacitors, diodes, BJTs, MOSFETs — schematic symbols and packages, and roughly what each does in a circuit.
  • Soldering: clean joints on through-hole and TQFP-scale SMD, desoldering with pump/wick/hot tweezers without lifting pads.
  • Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, used: not memorized — applied. You can look at two parallel resistors and know instantly what the meter across them will read.
  • Respect for mains: you know mains live parts bite and that stored charge in capacitors outlives the plug. If not, §3 is now your only allowed section.

Soft prerequisites

  • Oscilloscope basics — triggering, scale, probing without loading the circuit down.
  • LCR/ESR meter experience (your DE-5000-class instrument is ideal — the guide's cap-testing guidance leans on it).
  • Schematic reading at block-diagram level — power section, signal path, feedback loop.
  • Basic switch-mode supply concepts: chopper FET, transformer, feedback via optocoupler.

Self-assessment — all five, or fix the gap first

  1. A DMM in diode mode across a silicon diode reads 0.5xx V one way. What does it read the other way, and why?
  2. You measure 8 Ω across a big electrolytic on a motherboard VRM output. Short or not — and what single fact about the surrounding circuit decides?
  3. Why does a series light bulb prevent damage at first power-up of a repaired supply?
  4. A MOSFET's gate reads 0 V while its source-drain path should be conducting. Name two different reasons, one innocent and one guilty.
  5. Which capacitor failure modes does an ESR meter catch that a capacitance meter misses?
Check your answers diagnostic
  1. OL / open — the meter's test voltage is below the diode's reverse breakdown; only forward conduction registers. This asymmetry is why diode mode fingerprints junctions (§5).
  2. Usually not a short: you are reading the CPU die backwards through the buck coil (a coil is a wire). The deciding fact is whether the reading matches the board's normal rail fingerprint (§5, §7).
  3. The bulb's cold filament is low resistance; a fault draws huge current, the filament lights, its resistance rises, and voltage across the device collapses. A healthy device draws modest current and the bulb stays mostly dark (§3, §10).
  4. Innocent: a protection IC (BQ-family charger management) is deliberately holding gate drive off because it detected a downstream short. Guilty: the gate driver or its supply is dead (§6).
  5. Dried-out electros that still show correct capacitance but high series loss — ESR is the tell, capacitance alone says "fine" (§13, your LCR meter's D and ESR modes).

3 · Safety The floor under every other skill in this guide

Electronics repair mixes mains voltage, charged capacitors, and your hands. Mr. Carlson, the survey's high-voltage authority, treats safety as step zero on every line-powered job: isolation transformer before anything, discharge before fingers, gentle power-up while watching current. This section is not optional reading — it is the license for every lab that follows.

Lethal energy, three places
  • Mains itself: 120/230 VAC at tens of amps available. Across the chest, fatal.
  • Stored charge: SMPS primary caps hold 170–390 VDC and bite long after unplugging. Tube gear supplies hold 200–600 V.
  • Inverter/flash circuits: camera flashes, LCD backlights, microwave inverters — high voltage from small packages.

The three-tier hazard map

TierWhereHazardMandatory practice
Tier 1 · Low-voltage DCLogic boards, consoles, USB gear, battery railsParts damage, minor burns — rarely personal injuryCurrent-limited bench supply instead of battery/wall during diagnosis (§7)
Tier 2 · Mains-side SMPSPrimary side of any wall-powered supply, board-mounted or standalone170–390 VDC on primary caps; live chassis on the benchIsolation transformer, dim-bulb, discharge probe, one-hand rule
Tier 3 · Tube / high voltageVintage radios, tube amps, CRTs200–600 V supplies that stay charged for daysEverything in Tier 2, plus explicit discharge-and-verify before every reach-in, no exceptions, no hurry

The non-negotiables

  1. Isolation transformer for anything line-powered. Carlson's standing rule, stated as its own chapter in his fault-finding video. The transformer breaks the galvanic path between your device and the wall's ground reference, so touching a live chassis does not complete a circuit through you. Size it above your device's draw — 300 VA covers most bench SMPS and audio work.
  2. Discharge before you reach in. Bleeder the primary caps with a discharge probe (a resistor on a stick, not a screwdriver short — the spark pits the cap and your nerves). Then measure the cap to confirm zero. Carlson designed and sells his own HV discharge tool for exactly this sequence.
  3. Dim-bulb tester at first power-up. A series incandescent bulb between the isolation transformer and the device. Healthy device: bulb flashes, settles dim, device runs. Shorted device: bulb glows at full brightness, voltage across the device collapses, nothing burns. Build one in Lab 1.
  4. One-hand rule on live circuits. One hand in your pocket while probing energized gear. The current path you are choosing for a mistake is hand-to-hand — straight through the heart. Never two.
  5. Variac for gentle bring-up. Raise mains from low while watching current. Mark's AIWA deck was Japanese-market 100 V — the variac was not a safety luxury but the only correct supply. Know your device's nominal voltage before you dial anything.
  6. Never bypass a safety part silently. If you bridge a fusible resistor or defeat an interlock to test, tag it, and restore before return. A repair that removes a safety feature is a loan against someone's future injury.
Hot ground is not chassis ground Richard's rule from the small-SMPS guide: on non-isolated supplies (most cheap wall adapters, e-bike chargers, some TV supplies) the negative output is tied to mains through the rectifier — "hot ground." Clipping your grounded oscilloscope to it shorts half the mains waveform through the probe. Use an isolation transformer before any grounded instrument touches a non-isolated supply, or run the scope differential (two probes, channel math A−B) as Mark does on live rails.
Batteries are Tier 1.5 A laptop or console battery delivers hundreds of amps into a short — enough to burn tweezers and ignite pouch cells. For bench diagnosis, replace the battery with a current-limited supply. If the battery must stay in (battery-authentication circuits), tape over its terminals until the moment it is needed.

4 · Unified diagnostic method One spine, five accents — the loop every master runs

Here is the merge. Five channels, five eras of hardware, and when you strip the narration down to decisions, the same sequence appears every time. Learn this sequence as a checklist until it is reflex. The rest of the guide is this sequence run at depth: §5–§9 expand each phase, §10–§12 run it on specific hardware classes.

Why this ordering, and not "test the obvious suspects first"? Because the ordering is what makes evidence accumulate. Eyes before probes means you do not destroy the visual evidence. Power before signal means a "dead" signal stage is never investigated while the real fault is an absent rail upstream. Symmetry before bisection means you use the free reference channel before you start unsoldering half the board. Each phase either exits cleanly or hands the next phase a smaller bracket. Random part-testing hands the next phase nothing.
Where the masters genuinely differ Two real forks in the method, and both are gear-driven:
  • Finding shorts: Northridge reaches for the thermal camera plus voltage injection; Sorin demonstrates the same localization with a bench PSU and a wire (shorting coils one at a time, §7) and bans "short finder" gadgets on his forum. If you own a thermal cam, use it — it is fast. If you do not, Sorin's method gets you the same answer for the price of attention.
  • Repair philosophy: Carlson and Mark restore to original intent — correct parts, measured alignment, "works like new, or even better." Alex at Northridge fixes for commerce — a justified bridge of a dead protection FET can be a legitimate repair when the alternative is a dead board and an unhappy customer. This guide teaches both: restore-craft when the device deserves it, triage-craft when the job is paid by the outcome. §12 covers how to choose.

5 · Boards without schematics Read topology by eye, fingerprint rails by diode mode, sketch what you must

Most boards you will meet have no schematic, no boardview, and no manual — Sorin's Lenovo in the survey, Carlson's no-schematic SMPS, Richard's Dynacord mixer, Mark's deliberately obfuscated £25k phono stage. The masters do not treat this as an obstacle. A board is a printed argument about its own topology, and you can learn to read it in one slow pass.

Read the board by eye — the element table

Before any probe: turn the board under good light (and a microscope where you have one) and name what you see. Every structural element on a board is a topological statement:

Diode mode: the board's fingerprint

Diode mode is the repair world's stethoscope because it is safe (low test voltage, current-limited) and information-dense. Black probe on ground, red on the node, record the voltage drop. A board's set of rail readings is a fingerprint: learn the healthy one — from the good channel, a donor board, or experience — and faults read as outliers.

Diode mode reads both directions of wrong Alex's framing from the Switch liquid-damage repair: beginners only hunt the short (0 Ω). But OL where 0.4–0.5 V belongs is just as diagnostic — it means the path is blown open: a vaporized filter, a corroded via, a lifted pad. Fingerprint both ways.

Reverse-engineer when the board is not enough

When a fault resists the eye and the fingerprint, reconstruct the circuit — this is the LER signature skill and Mark's fallback on the obfuscated phono stage:

  1. Photograph the board region at high resolution, both sides. You will forget the layout; the photo will not.
  2. Identify the functional block (input stage, output stage, buck converter, feedback loop) around the symptom. Sketch its parts on paper with reference designators.
  3. Trace connectivity with continuity mode — pad to pad, pin to pin. Mark suggested a sketch of the whole PCB with parts labeled when the control chip is unobtainable; the sketch is the schematic.
  4. Test assumptions against the working channel wherever one exists — every deduced connection on the bad side must match the good side.
  5. Annotate voltages as you measure them onto the sketch. The sketch becomes a service manual; Mark literally wrote one for the "unfixable" pre-amp and fixed it.
Component identity without markings Richard's unmarked/blown-IC video exists for the worst case: the faulty chip has no readable marking. Identify by role, not by name — package, pin count, what it connects to (a 8-pin part between port and PMIC with ESD neighbors is a port mux/protector), and behavior in diode mode. A part that cannot be identified can still be identified by its job, and replaced by anything that does that job on the same footprint.

6 · Power entry & rails Walk the chain, watch the gates, trust the fuse last

A dead device is usually a device whose power never arrived. Sorin's most-watched repair (2.63M views) is one walk of one chain, and the walk generalizes to every laptop, console, and battery gadget you will ever meet: port → fuse → protection FETs → sense resistor → main rail. Voltage exists at every node or it does not. Where it stops, the fault lives — and only then do parts become suspects.

The walk, as procedure

  1. Verify the source. Adapter output at the port under no load, then with the meter watching while you connect. A dead or dying adapter is the cheapest fault you will ever "diagnose."
  2. Walk node to node. Port → any input inductor → fuse → FET 1 → FET 2 → sense resistor → main-rail caps. Voltage at each. The fault is bracketed between the last good node and the first dead one.
  3. Gate voltages before part removal. A FET with a good drain-source path but 0 V on its gate is being told to stay off. Check the gate driver, the BQ IC, and what they are reacting to downstream (Sorin's Toshiba case: gate held at 0.9 V on purpose; the second FET was shorted).
  4. Inject where voltage died. Bench supply, current-limited, feeding the main rail directly (ERS's "simple test"): if the machine powers on, everything downstream is exonerated and the fault is confined to the input chain.
  5. Then, and only then, the 3.3 V / 5 V rails. Sorin's rule from the HP repair: "no point checking the 3.3 when I don't have the 19." Rails are a dependency chain; test in dependency order.
The fuse never saves the board Sorin: the fuse blows after the main MOSFET has already shorted — it protects the wiring, not the electronics. A blown fuse is a symptom with a cause upstream of it. Replace it, and if it blows again (Alex's HP Spectre repair — on camera), you have been handed a free short-localization event, not a repair failure.

Firmware before hardware

One whole class of "dead" boards is corrupt firmware. Sorin's HP no-picture case: Caps Lock blinking twice (when it blinks at all), recovery via the vendor's USB BIOS recovery utility with Win+B held through power-on — a free seven-minute fix for a fault that looks like motherboard death. Before board surgery on any no-picture/no-boot machine: vendor BIOS recovery first. A cheap USB BIOS-tester dongle (red LED = chip powered, flashing green = data lines talking) confirms the flash chip is alive in seconds.

7 · Short hunting Taxonomy first, then current-limited injection — thermal camera optional

A short is a fault with a personality: it draws current, drops voltage, and — if you feed it carefully — gets warm. The masters hunt it in three moves: classify the short, localize it to a rail, then localize it to a part. What they refuse to do is poke randomly at caps with a meter and pray. And the survey's best news: Sorin's coil-shorting method finds shorts with a bench PSU and a piece of wire — no thermal camera required.

Taxonomy — what shorts and what does not

ComponentShort behaviorWhy
Ceramic / decoupling capsHard 0 Ω shortCracked die, transient over-voltage; sheer numbers make them the statistical favorite
MOSFETs (high-side especially)Low-but-not-zero resistanceFailed die leaves partial conduction — why thermal methods see them easily
ICs (chargers, PMICs, muxes)Low resistance on their rail pinAlex's prior: "it's usually the chip, not the cap" on BQ-adjacent shorts
ResistorsNever a shortAlex, flatly: "resistors do not cause a short." Exclude them from the suspect list entirely
Solder bridges, loose screws, corrosionAnything, anywhereThe inspection-first pass of §4 phase 1 exists to catch these for free

The hunt — injection, then two localization methods

  1. Feed the shorted rail from a bench supply, hard current-limited. Sorin's safety math: inject 0.8–1 V, limit ~500 mA. CPUs and GPUs draw tens of amps in normal operation — half an amp at one volt cannot hurt them, and the shorted rail will pull the supply into current limit and hold it there.
  2. Localize — method A, thermal (if you own the camera): whichever part warms first is dissipating the injected power. Alex's HP Spectre flow: inject 1.2 V, watch the thermal image, done.
  3. Localize — method B, coil-shorting (a wire and attention): with power injected on the main rail, short each VRM coil (phase output) to ground one at a time with tweezers while watching the PSU's voltage readout. Healthy rail: no change — its high-side FET is off and the body diode blocks any path from your injected voltage to the phase node. Guilty rail: the voltage collapses — the fault already connects that rail to ground, and shorting its coil swaps the short's partial resistance (~6 Ω) for 0 Ω, slamming the supply fully into current limit. The readout names the rail. For suspected high-side FET shorts, short the coil to +V instead of ground and the same logic tests the FET's own drain-source path.
  4. Bisect the rail. Pull an inductor or FET mid-rail to split the net into halves (Alex's Spectre bisect; Mark's regulator-output disconnect). The half that still loads the supply carries the short. Repeat.
  5. Condemn out of circuit. The last part standing gets pulled and confirmed: a shorted FET reads drain-source ohms both directions on the bench; a shorted cap reads 0 Ω alone on the table. In-circuit parallel paths are done lying to you.
  6. Repair and re-fingerprint. Replace, re-inject gently, confirm the rail now holds voltage at sensible current, then diode-mode the rail to verify the fingerprint returned to normal.
Why coil-shorting works — the impedance argument Your injected voltage reaches a phase node only through that rail's high-side FET. FET off: the body diode blocks, so shorting a healthy coil to ground changes nothing — no path, no extra current, readout steady. But the shorted rail already has a path (a shorted FET is a wire; a shorted cap behind the coil is a resistor), so its rail was already drawing current and pulling the supply toward its limit. Adding a direct 0 Ω coil-to-ground short across that rail's remaining impedance drops the total load to zero ohms — the supply pins at its current limit and the voltage visibly collapses. The method is Kirchhoff with a tweezer. (Sorin's video walks the same logic for high-side FET shorts: short the coil to +V instead of ground.)
Beware the "short finder" gadget Sorin has the phrase banned on his own Discord. Milliohm meters and short-finder boxes promise localization but fail exactly where you need them — dense multilayer boards with parallel paths. The bench supply plus the two methods above outperforms them at zero extra cost.

When the short fights back

Alex's HP Spectre case is the canonical cautionary tale: short located, BQ IC and both MOSFETs replaced from a donor — and the fresh MOSFETs shorted again on power-up. Second attempt made, second failure documented, and then the professional's exit: no-fix, board returned, customer informed. A short that kills replacement parts is telling you the damage is wider than the bracket (dead silicon elsewhere, internal plane damage) or the replacement parts were bad (mark his other lesson: donor parts get diode-tested before fitting). Two strikes, then a hard look at the economics. §12 formalizes the decision.

8 · Signal-chain tracing When power is fine but the function is gone

Rails up, board alive, function missing: no sound, no picture, no data, no deflection. Now the fault is in a signal path, and Carlson's method owns this territory: inject a known-good signal at the input and walk it stage by stage until it dies. The stage where it dies hands you a two-component bracket, and the good channel — when one exists — is your free reference for what the signal should look like at every point.

The trace, as procedure

  1. Quantify the symptom before opening the case. Mark's phono-stage repair starts with the scope: buzz on one channel, ripple period measured at 8 ms — 125 Hz, not 50/60 Hz. "That's not mains hum." One measurement killed the most popular wrong hypothesis before the screwdriver came out.
  2. Verify power to every stage first. Signal tracing on a stage with a collapsed rail is wasted motion (his +27 V rail was down — find that before touching the signal path).
  3. Inject a known-good signal at the chain's input. Audio: tone or music from a generator or a known-good source. Radio: Carlson feeds the antenna/IF from a generator. Use the real input whenever possible — some faults live in the input jack itself.
  4. Walk the chain, stage by stage. Probe each stage's output: Carlson's sequence on a radio is antenna → mixer/oscillator → IF → detector → audio, checking that the signal exists and grows as it should at each hop. The first stage whose output is dead or distorted owns the fault.
  5. Diff against the twin. Stereo? Mark strips the working channel's numbers at every test point and compares. Component-for-component, voltage-for-voltage. The fault is by definition an asymmetry.
  6. Probe live rails differentially. Mark's scope technique on the Beocord: two probes, channel math A−B, never grounding a live rail with a grounded scope. Or isolate the device (§3) and probe normally.
  7. Confirm the condemned part out of circuit (§4 phase 7), repair, then re-walk the whole chain — a stage you "fixed" can reveal the next fault only when signal flows through it again.

The usual mechanical suspects

  • Belts: turned to goo or slack. Replace with correct cross-section, not "close enough." Old belt residue dissolves with isopropyl before the new belt goes on.
  • Idler tires and pinch rollers: hardened, glazed, undersize. Mark sanded an oversize replacement pinch roller to size rather than waiting for NOS parts.
  • Bearings and bushes: noise that quiets under finger pressure on the shaft = end-play or dry bearing; oil and run overnight to work it in. Worn egg-shaped pivot holes get re-bushed — clock-repair bushes, hand-reamed in 0.1 mm steps (Beocord).
  • Grease gone to glue: thirty-year-old grease is a brake. Clean out, relube with the right grade for the speed of the part.
  • Switches, relays, pots: oxide films. Ultrasonic tank, contact cleaner, dry thoroughly. Mark dries in an oven; Carlson's radio work treats every switch as a suspect until exercised under load.
  • Connector fretting and cracked solder: any connector that carries mechanical stress (headphone jacks, barrel jacks) primes its solder joints for fracture — inspect, reflow, and mechanically support.
Measure mystery parts — never trust the markings Mark's most-shared own-goal: a part reading "883 Ω" on the schematic-fragment of his mind was actually a 10 nF capacitor (measured 12 nF) — his resistor replacement sent the motor full-speed. Two lessons, both his: measure unknown parts before replacing them, and an honest repairer narrates the mistake. If your method cannot survive being wrong once, it is not a method.

10 · SMPS deep dive The one topology class that crosses every era of hardware

Switch-mode supplies are the common denominator of repair: they fail most, they kill most, and they appear in every device class this guide covers. Carlson's no-schematic supply video teaches the master move — identify the topology first, and the failure modes follow. Richard's SMPS guide supplies the failure taxonomy. Together with Mark's rail-first discipline, this module walks the whole supply, hot side to cold side, with a meter and respect.

The supply walk

  1. Cold side first: diode-mode the outputs. Output rails shorted? That drags the primary down or keeps the supply in protection. Fingerprint every output before anything else.
  2. Unplug. Discharge. Verify discharged. The primary cap holds 170–390 VDC. This is not a step you do when rushed — it is the step that decides whether there is a rushed afternoon after this one.
  3. Fuse, NTC, EMI parts: continuity each. A blown fuse means a hot-side event — find it before replacing (§6: the fuse blows after the fault).
  4. Bridge rectifier in diode mode: four junctions, each ~0.5 V forward, OL reverse. Shorted bridge legs are a classic dead-short across mains.
  5. Chopper FET out-of-circuit: gate-source and drain-source both directions. The single most-failed hot-side part; test it on the bench, not in the board.
  6. PWM controller and its startup cap: Richard's taxonomy — see the table below. Measure the startup cap's ESR/capacitance before trusting it.
  7. Rectifier and filter on the cold side: diode-mode the secondary rectifier; ESR the output caps. High-ripple complaints usually end here.
  8. Dim-bulb first power-up. Flash-then-dim = healthy startup. Steady bright = short still present. Repeat the walk.

Richard's startup-capacitor failure family

The small cap that feeds the PWM controller until the auxiliary winding takes over fails in ways that look like four different faults. From the LER SMPS guide's own taxonomy:

Topology first, then failure modes — Carlson's no-schematic move You do not need the schematic. Identify the converter class from the board (§5's element table): one switch and one transformer winding visible → flyback (the wall-wart majority); two switches driving a transformer with no output inductor → half-bridge (Carlson's case); switches plus output inductor → forward/buck-derived. Each class has a personality: flybacks kill their chopper FET and startup caps; half-bridges lose their switches in pairs and shift drive timing; both die at the primary cap when someone works without discharging. Name the class, and the fault list writes itself.
Hot-side probing rules Isolation transformer mandatory (§3). Ground-referenced instruments clip to COLD ground only — the hot side's "ground" is a moving waveform, and a grounded scope probe on it is a fault you create. For hot-side waveforms: differential probing (two probes, A−B math) or an isolated handheld scope. Richard's one-line version: hot ground is not chassis ground.

11 · Consoles & USB-C The commercial mainstream: Switch, PS5, and everything with a rounded port

This is the device class that pays commercial rent: Nintendo Switch no-charge, PS5 HDMI, laptop USB-C dead. The unified method runs unchanged, but the priors shift — liquid damage and port abuse outrank component aging, and a small set of named ICs absorbs most of the probability. Northridge Fix's Switch and PS5 work in the survey is the map.

The Switch no-charge triage, as taught by Alex's liquid-damage repair

  1. Microscope pass over the known weak points before any probe: USB-C port pins, battery connector, Joy-Con connectors, then the IC neighborhoods — BQ (charging), M92 (protection), MAX (PMIC), PI3USB ("P13", the port mux), LCD connector. Liquid leaves crust exactly there.
  2. Diode-mode the data lines. Healthy: ~0.4–0.5 V drop each. Zero where a drop belongs = short. OL where a drop belongs = blown-open filter or cracked line. Both are liquid classics.
  3. Follow the corrosion. Discolored coil or IC perimeter is testimony — the customer's "it just stopped" becomes a chemistry story. Photograph it; you will want the before photo when you write the ticket (§12).
  4. Replace filters and caps from a donor board (donor parts diode-tested first — always). Protect adjacent connectors with silicone pads and a narrow nozzle during hot-air work.
  5. Prove the charge path on the bench. Watch USB-PD negotiate on a PD-capable supply: request at 12 V, settle at 9 V / ~1.7 A = healthy negotiation and real charging current, not just "icon appears."

Port and pad repair — the PS5 HDMI masterclass

Alex's PS5 disaster repair is the survey's rework-craft lesson. The prior tech's failed port job left ~8 missing pads and loose fragments — "8 out of 10 PlayStations we get in now have prior repair attempts." The recovery:

  1. Catalogue every missing pad before touching the iron. Sort them by continuity-to-ground: only signal pads get jumper strips; ground pads just need re-connection.
  2. Board-level repair first, connector second. Replace the missing cap and diode before reflowing the new port — "otherwise you're creating more work for yourself."
  3. Jumper strips: pre-tinned copper from surviving trace to pad position, extended and sealed under UV solder mask.
  4. Reflow with intent: nozzle direction matters more than the dial ("it's not the temperature, it's where you point the nozzle"); press and hold 5–10 s for the settle.
  5. Verify like a spectator: tug-test every pin with a needle, check for bridges, re-inspect. "The person looking from the outside can see more than the person working on the board" — re-inspect your own work as if it were someone else's.
Donor boards are the commercial parts catalog Alex's shop keeps dead boards by model; Sorin pulls input FETs from spares (they barely switch — substitution risk is low); Mark's Yamaha rebuild needed six gain-matched outputs and the Crown wanted a near-sibling's manual. A donor board is a schematic, a reference fingerprint, and a parts bin in one object. Buy dead boards of the models you service most.
USB-C is a negotiation, not a socket A USB-C device that will not charge with a known-good cable and brick is usually failing negotiation, not conducting badly: the CC-line resistors, the port mux (PI3USB class), the PD controller, or their ESD filters. Diode-mode the CC and data lines first — before ordering a port you may not need. And always test with more than one known-good charger/cable pair: the fault must be shown to live in the device, not in the customer's pocket lint or $3 cable.

12 · Going commercial Triage, time-boxing, and the no-fix that keeps you in business

Commercial repair is the same electronics with a clock and a customer attached. Northridge Fix is the survey's model here: mail-in volume, paying jobs, and the discipline that separates a shop from a hobby with a queue — time-boxed diagnosis, honest no-fixes, and documentation that protects both sides. Richard's industrial-repair video adds the other half: "fix just about anything for profit" means choosing jobs where your method beats the replacement price.

The commercial loop

  1. Intake as contract. Record the customer's exact words, what came with the device, and its condition photos — before you power it. Alex's Switch ticket: "customer wrote nothing else." The vague ticket is itself data: expect surprises, price accordingly.
  2. Triage on the clock. The fixed order (eyes → diode mode → thermal → injection) is fast because it is fixed. A commercial triage pass on a no-power laptop is 15–30 minutes to a bracketed fault; that is a diagnosable, quotable unit of work.
  3. Quote at the bracket. Once the fault is localized, the repair scope is known: parts (donor or ordered), rework time, risk. Quote after diagnosis, not before — and charge for diagnosis either way. "No fix, no fee is never an option" (Alex) — free diagnosis is a charity with your name on it.
  4. Time-box the rabbit holes. New parts that die on power-up twice = document, stop, return. "I cannot afford to spend the whole day working on one single board." The no-fix letter is a professional product: what was found, what was tried, what it would take.
  5. Repair for the job, not for the museum. Restore-craft (Carlson/Mark) for gear worth restoring; triage-craft (bridge the dead bypass cap — its siblings still filter the rail) for volume work. Both are legitimate. Silent shortcuts are not: every bypass or defeated protection goes in the notes.
  6. Verify like a skeptic, document like a lawyer. Real loads, real currents, burn-in. Then the ticket: symptom, cause, parts changed, evidence, advice to the customer. Your future self inherits every job you document and every job you don't.

    15 · Labs Skills are built on the bench, not on the couch

    Every lab below uses dead or disposable hardware, mains safety where mains is involved, and ends at a checkpoint you can verify. Work them in order — each assumes the previous one's skill. Practice is not the appendix to this guide; it is the guide. The reading is just the license to practice.

    Lab 1 · Build and use the dim-bulb tester
    45 minutes + drying time · mains involved · materials: junction box, incandescent socket+bulb 60–100 W, IEC in/out or flex · from §3
    1. Wire the bulb in SERIES between input and output per the §3 diagram. In a proper enclosure, strain relief on every cord, no exposed conductors. Label it.
    2. Test with a known-GOOD low-power device (a lamp or small radio, not a motor):
    3. Expect: bulb flashes at power-on, settles dim or dark; device runs normally.
    4. Test the short case with a sacrificial extension lead wired socket-to-plug-backwards (or simply trust the physics after step 3):
    5. Expect: on a deliberate short, the bulb snaps to full brightness and the output voltage across the short collapses to a few volts. Nothing sparks. That is the whole point.
    Checkpoint: you can state, before connecting any future repaired device, which bulb behavior means "healthy" (flash-then-dim), which means "startup surge" (brief bright, decays), and which means "do not proceed" (steady full glow).
    Lab 2 · Fingerprint a healthy board
    30 minutes · Tier 1 only (battery disconnected) · dead laptop or console board from eBay/junk drawer · from §5
    1. Photograph the board both sides. Battery disconnected, no power applied — this lab is meter-only.
    2. Identify by eye and annotate on the photo: DC-in port, fuse, input FET pair, sense resistor, main rail caps, every VRM coil, BIOS chip, battery connector.
    3. Diode mode, black on ground: measure and record the drop at — the DC-in pad, the main rail, each VRM output (at its coil), and 3–4 data lines at any USB/port connector.
    4. Expect: data lines ~0.4–0.5 V; rails a spread of OL, diode-ish, and low-ohm readings. The low ohms on VRM outputs is the CPU die through the coil — write down the values; this table is your fingerprint of "normal" for this model.
    5. Re-measure three readings to see your own repeatability (probe pressure and pad oxidation move numbers more than beginners expect).
    Checkpoint: you own a written, repeatable fingerprint table for one model, and you can point at the board and name every element of §5's table in the flesh.
    Lab 3 · Rail substitution on a dead board
    30 minutes · Tier 1 · same board as Lab 2 (or any junk laptop board) · bench PSU · from §6
    1. Set the bench PSU to the board's main-rail voltage (19 V typical for laptops — check the adapter's label), current limit to 0.5 A.
    2. Clip to the main-rail cap pads (or the sense-resistor output side). Power on the supply.
    3. Expect: a healthy-ish board draws a trickle and may wake slightly (fans twitch on some models). A board with a short pins the supply at 0.5 A with the voltage dragged low.
    4. If the voltage drags: you have a live short-hunt subject for Lab 4. Note the voltage it settles at — lower = harder short.
    5. Practice ERS's "simple test" logic in reverse: inject at the main rail only after confirming nothing at the input chain, and always through the current limit.
    Checkpoint: you can state what the board drew, whether that meant short or sleep, and why the current limit made the experiment safe.
    Lab 4 · Coil-shorting short hunt
    30–45 minutes · Tier 1 · the shorted board from Lab 3 (or source a known-dead one) · bench PSU, tweezers · from §7
    1. Drop the PSU to 1 V, limit 0.5 A (Sorin's safety math — silicon cannot be hurt at half an amp, one volt). Reconnect to the main rail.
    2. Short each VRM coil's output to ground, one at a time, with tweezers while watching the PSU voltage readout.
    3. Expect: most rails — no change (FET off, body diode blocks). The shorted rail — readout collapses toward zero as the limit engages.
    4. Bisect: pull the coil (or the FET) of the guilty rail. Half the net still loads the supply? The short is upstream of the break; not? Downstream. One removal = half the board exonerated.
    5. Converge to a part, pull it, and confirm out of circuit: 0 Ω alone on the bench = guilty. Nothing else gets replaced.
    Checkpoint: named rail, named part, out-of-circuit proof — with zero parts replaced except the guilty one. That is phase 4→7 of the method, executed.
    Lab 5 · Capacitor survey with your LCR meter
    40 minutes · Tier 0 (unpowered bench work) · any old audio board or supply, 10+ electrolytics · DE-5000-class LCR · from §9, §13
    1. Pick ten electrolytics. For each: photograph markings (value, voltage, series), then measure capacitance AND D/ESR out of circuit at 100 Hz/1 kHz as your meter's manual directs.
    2. Expect: most read within tolerance with modest D. Any with high D or C far off marking — especially in switching positions — is how dried-out caps announce themselves while capacitance-only meters say "fine."
    3. Compare two same-value caps from different board regions (hot switching supply vs cool audio path) — aging tracks heat.
    4. Re-test one in circuit, then out: see exactly how parallel paths corrupt the in-circuit number. That is §4 phase 7's out-of-circuit rule, felt.
    Checkpoint: a ten-row table of value/D/ESR with at least one cap condemned on D despite passing on C — and you can explain to a customer why that cap was replaced.
    Lab 6 · Signal-chain diff on stereo gear
    60–90 minutes · Tier 1/2 (mains via your new dim-bulb + isolation) · thrift-store stereo amp, one channel fault optional · scope + signal source · from §8
    1. Safety floor first: isolation transformer, dim-bulb in series, one hand habits (§3). No speaker connected yet — dummy load or open outputs for now.
    2. Inject a 1 kHz tone at the input. Scope the good channel: input jack, each stage's test point, the output. Write the amplitudes down — you are writing the reference table.
    3. Walk the bad channel identically. The first test point where its numbers diverge from the reference brackets the fault.
    4. Measure DC voltages of both channels at that stage — asymmetric DC on a symmetric circuit is a confession.
    5. Diode-test the semiconductors in the bracketed stage out of circuit. ESR its electrolytics (Lab 5 skill).
    Checkpoint: fault bracketed to one stage with recorded evidence on both channels. If you found and fixed the part: verify DC offset < 10 mV at the output and re-walk the chain end to end before declaring victory.

    16 · Capstone A dead wall-powered device, cold side to hot side, end to end

    The full method, one object, no skipping. Acquire a dead mains-powered device with an internal SMPS — a thrift-store boombox, a dead external hard-drive enclosure, a wall-wart with a circuit board inside, or an ATX supply. You will run every phase of §4 with the safety floor of §3 always under you. Budget an afternoon, not an hour.

    Capstone · Full workup of a dead SMPS device
    3–4 hours · Tier 2 — mains-side work · everything from the labs · deliverable: a repair ticket

    Phase 1 — Intake & eyes (20 min)

    1. Write the ticket yourself as if from a customer: device, symptom, history unknown.
    2. Open, photograph as-found both sides, and catalogue: bulging/vented caps, darkened resistors, corrosion, prior repairs, the §5 element table applied for real.

    Phase 2 — Safety floor (10 min, non-negotiable)

    1. Unplug. Discharge the primary cap through a resistor. Measure it at zero before every reach-in from here on.
    2. Rig isolation transformer + dim-bulb. From now on, the device never sees bare mains.

    Phase 3–4 — Cold side, then the walk (45 min)

    1. Diode-mode every output rail — fingerprint before theory.
    2. Short on an output? Run the §7 hunt at low voltage from your bench supply (cold side only — safe Tier 1 work): localize, bisect, condemn out of circuit.
    3. Outputs clean? Walk §10's supply order: fuse, NTC, bridge junctions, chopper FET out-of-circuit, PWM startup cap on the LCR meter.

    Phase 5–7 — Fix and prove (60–90 min)

    1. Replace exactly the condemned part(s), correct rating, correct orientation (§8 phase 8 discipline).
    2. Re-fingerprint the repaired node diode-mode. Compare to your written table.

    Phase 8–9 — Bring-up and burn-in (45 min)

    1. Dim-bulb power-up. Flash-then-dim: proceed to the variac (if you own one) rising to nominal while watching current.
    2. Verify outputs under a real load (resistor or the actual device): voltage within spec, ripple on the scope < the cap's promise, nothing warm that should not be.
    3. Burn-in 30 minutes under load. Re-check ripple at the end — caps that fail hot fail here, on your bench, instead of in a customer's hands.

    Phase 10 — The ticket (15 min)

    1. Write it like §12 teaches: symptom, evidence trail (your photos and tables), cause, parts replaced, verification data, advice. An honest failed capstone — no-fix, well-documented — is also a pass, if the ticket shows the method.
    Pass criteria: every phase left written evidence; exactly the guilty parts were replaced; the device (or the documented no-fix) survived honest verification; and you could hand the ticket to another tech who could follow your reasoning start to finish.

    18 · Cheat sheet One column, bench-height — the method in cards

    The universal starting pattern

    • Read ticket → photograph as-found → microscope pass
    • Safety floor: isolate · discharge · verify zero · dim-bulb staged
    • Diode-mode fingerprint the rails (write the numbers down)
    • Power entry walk: port → fuse → FETs → sense → main rail
    • Gates before parts. Rails in dependency order. Firmware before hardware.
    • Bracket → halve → condemn out of circuit → fix → verify like a skeptic

    Safety, every mains job

    • Isolation transformer for anything line-powered — no exceptions
    • Discharge primary caps through a resistor; measure zero after
    • Dim-bulb at first power-up: flash-dim good, steady glow = stop
    • One hand in pocket on live circuits
    • Grounded instruments clip to cold ground only — hot ground is not chassis ground
    • Batteries out for diagnosis; current-limited supply in

    Diode-mode fingerprint (black on ground)

    • Data line healthy: ~0.4–0.5 V drop
    • 0.000 V on a rail: dead short — hunt caps/ICs (FETs partial)
    • OL where a drop belongs: blown-open path
    • Low ohms at VRM output: the CPU die through the coil — usually normal
    • Resistors never short (Alex) — exclude them
    • "Usually the chip, not the cap" near BQ circuits

    Short hunt, no thermal camera

    • Bench PSU on the rail: 1 V, 0.5 A limit (safe for silicon)
    • Short each coil to ground; guilty rail = voltage collapses
    • High-side FET suspect: short coil to +V instead
    • Bisect by pulling coils/FETs — half the board exonerated per pull
    • Converge, pull, prove out of circuit, replace only that
    • Parts dying twice → time-box, document, no-fix (§12)

    Gates & priors

    • FET "off"? Check gate voltage and what's holding it before pulling it
    • BQ/protection IC pulls gate drive on detected faults — the FET is innocent
    • Fuse blows after the fault, never before it — keep hunting upstream
    • No-picture machine: BIOS recovery USB before board surgery
    • Switch no-charge order: port → M92 → BQ → PI3USB → MAX

    Signal & analogue

    • Rails to every stage before signal tracing
    • Inject known-good input; walk stage by stage until it dies
    • Stereo: the good channel is your free reference — diff everything
    • Hum: measure the period, name the source (8 ms = 125 Hz ≠ mains)
    • Live-rail probing: differential (two probes, A−B) or isolate first
    • Transport dead: mark the pulley, watch it turn — mechanical first

    SMPS quick table

    • Cold side first: fingerprint outputs, ESR the output caps
    • Never starts / starts-dies / chirps / temp-dependent: the startup cap family
    • Chopper FET = most-failed hot-side part — out of circuit only
    • Name the topology (flyback/half-bridge) and the failure list writes itself
    • Primary cap = 170–390 V stored. Discharge. Verify. Every time.

    Pro habits

    • Photograph before, during, after — as-found beats memory
    • Donor parts get diode-tested before fitting
    • Measure mystery parts; markings lie (the 883 Ω capacitor)
    • Re-inspect your work like a spectator (Alex)
    • Time-box rabbit holes; a documented no-fix is a deliverable
    • Ticket: symptom → evidence → cause → parts → verification → advice

    19 · Glossary Every term of art, with where it was taught

    Bisection
    Splitting a fault domain in half by disconnecting at a boundary (pulling a coil, FET, or connector) and testing which half carries the fault. One pull exonerates half the board. (§7)
    Bracketing
    Confining a fault between a last-known-good node and a first-known-bad node. The fundamental unit of diagnostic progress. (§1)
    BQ IC
    TI's battery/charger-management family (BQ24xxx etc.). Watches the input chain's sense lines and deliberately pulls protection-FET gate drive when it detects a fault — the innocent reason a FET reads "off." (§6)
    Cold side / hot side
    The two halves of an SMPS split by the transformer's isolation boundary. Cold = output-referenced and (usually) safe; hot = mains-referenced, stores lethal charge in the primary cap. (§10)
    Dim-bulb tester
    A series incandescent lamp between mains and a device. Cold filament = low loss; fault current lights it and its rising resistance collapses the voltage across the device. Flash-then-dim = healthy startup; steady glow = short. (§3)
    Diode mode
    DMM mode reporting forward voltage drop across a junction (≈0.5–0.7 V silicon), OL when reverse or absent. The safe, information-dense default for fingerprinting boards. (§5)
    Fingerprint (rail)
    The set of diode-mode readings at a board's rails and data lines when healthy, used as the reference for spotting outliers. Built from a twin channel, donor board, or records. (§5)
    Flyback / half-bridge
    Converter topologies. Flyback: one switch, energy stored in the transformer itself — the wall-wart majority. Half-bridge: two switches alternately driving the transformer — common in bigger supplies, notorious for losing switches in pairs. (§10)
    Fusible resistor
    A resistor designed to fail open on overload, protecting what follows. Mark checks these first after any blow-up — "they're designed to blow," so they lead you to the event. (§9, §6)
    Gate (MOSFET)
    The control terminal. Gate voltage decides conduction; measuring it before pulling a FET distinguishes a dead FET from a healthy one being deliberately held off. "The gate pin is the gatekeeper" (Alex). (§6)
    Hot ground
    The "ground" of a non-isolated supply's output, sitting at a fraction of mains potential. Clipping a grounded instrument to it creates a real fault. Isolate the device first. (§3)
    Voltage injection
    Feeding a rail from a current-limited bench supply to substitute missing power, localize shorts (watch current/heat), or isolate fault domains. The bench PSU's core job. (§7)
    M92 / PI3USB / MAX PMIC
    Named ICs on Nintendo Switch-class boards: M92 = input protection; PI3USB ("P13") = USB-C port mux; MAX = power-management IC. Standard first suspects in no-charge triage. (§11)
    No-fix
    The professional outcome where a repair is stopped at its time-box and returned documented, not "fixed." A deliverable, not a defeat. (§12)
    Octopus (VI tracer)
    A junk-box instrument: signal source + two resistors + scope in X-Y, drawing a component's V-I curve live. Shorted junctions look nothing like healthy ones. Richard's DIY doctrine in one object. (§8)
    ESR / D
    Equivalent series resistance / dissipation factor — loss metrics that catch dried-out electrolytics whose capacitance still reads in tolerance. D and ESR modes on your LCR meter. (§9, Lab 5)
    Primary capacitor
    The main energy-storage cap after the bridge rectifier on an SMPS hot side: 170–390 VDC when charged, holding it long after unplugging. Discharge and verify before every reach-in. (§10)
    PWM controller
    The switching-supply brain, fed at startup by the small startup capacitor whose failure family (never starts / starts-dies / chirps / temperature-dependent) mimics four different faults. (§10)
    Sense resistor
    The milliohm shunt between protection FETs and the main rail; the BQ IC watches its voltage to decide whether the input chain is healthy. A node on the §6 walk. (§6)
    Symmetry method (twin diff)
    Using a duplicated structure — stereo channel, twin module — as a free known-good reference. Every measurement on the bad side gets a good-side comparison. Mark's core move. (§4, §8)
    Topology recognition
    Identifying a circuit's class (flyback, buck, half-bridge, output stage) from board structure alone — the skill that replaces missing schematics. "The schematic is a convenience; topology knowledge is the real instrument." (§5, §10)
    VRM
    Voltage-regulator module: the multiphase buck converters (coil + FET pair per phase) feeding CPU/GPU. Largest coils on a modern board; its high-side FETs are the most common dead-short silicon. (§5, §7)

    21 · Quiz Sixteen flashcards, self-scored — aim for 13/16

    Score: 0 learned · 0 review · 0/16 answered
    Q1A DMM reads 8 Ω across a motherboard VRM output. What is it almost certainly measuring, and why?
    The CPU/GPU die's resistance through the buck coil — a coil is just wire, and current takes the lowest-resistance path, so the meter sees the silicon, not a fault. Compare to a known-good rail before calling anything shorted. (§5, §7)
    Q2What does a blown fuse on a dead laptop actually tell you — and what is the very next move?
    The fuse blew after the fault — it protects wiring, not electronics (Sorin). Next move: find what killed it. Walk the input chain and short-hunt; a fuse that blows again on camera is free fault localization, not failure. (§6)
    Q3An input MOSFET shows 19 V on its drain and 0 V on its gate. Two explanations — one innocent, one guilty?
    Innocent: a BQ/protection IC is deliberately holding gate drive off because it detected a downstream fault — fix the fault, not the FET. Guilty: the gate driver or its supply is dead. Measure what controls the gate before pulling the FET. (§6)
    Q4Name the injection limits Sorin uses when hunting shorts, and why they are safe for silicon.
    0.8–1 V, current-limited to ~500 mA. CPUs/GPUs normally draw tens of amps at sub-ohm loads — half an amp through a short cannot damage the die, and low voltage cannot forward-bias anything destructively. (§7)
    Q5During coil-shorting, what does the PSU readout do on a healthy rail versus the shorted rail — and what single component property explains the difference?
    Healthy rail: no change — its high-side FET is off and the body diode blocks the path. Shorted rail: voltage collapses as the supply pins at its current limit — because the fault already provided a path, and your 0 Ω short removes its residual resistance. Impedance to ground is the property. (§7)
    Q6Diode mode on a USB data line reads OL. Short or not — and what does it mean instead?
    Not a short — the opposite. Data lines should read ~0.4–0.5 V; OL means the path is blown open: vaporized ESD filter, cracked trace, lifted pad. Diode mode reads both directions of wrong. (§5, §11)
    Q7What are the three bulb behaviors at first power-up of a repaired supply, and what does each mean?
    Flash-then-dim: healthy startup surge. Brief bright decaying: legitimate inrush on some supplies (Richard's caveat). Steady full glow: short still present — stop and re-walk. (§3, §10)
    Q8An audio channel buzzes. Mark scopes it: 8 ms period. Why does that one measurement change the entire diagnosis?
    8 ms = 125 Hz, which is not 50/60 Hz mains hum — so the popular "ground loop or mains ripple" hypothesis dies immediately, and the hunt goes to rectifier/switching-related ripple or a collapsing rail instead. Measure the period; name the source. (§8)
    Q9Tape deck transport is dead. Name Mark's first diagnostic move — no meter required.
    Mark the motor pulley with a marker, run the mechanism, and watch. Pulley turns, tape doesn't: belts/idler (mechanical). Pulley still: motor or drive electronics. Split mechanical from electrical before probing. (§9)
    Q10List the startup-capacitor failure family in an SMPS — four symptoms, one part.
    Never starts (cap open — controller never reaches start threshold); starts then shuts down (reaches start, not sustain); pulsing/chirping (audible retry loop); starts only cold or only hot (ESR/capacitance drift at the threshold). (§10)
    Q11Why does Carlson signal-trace instead of recapping a radio that "sounds bad" — what does the method buy that shotgunning cannot?
    Stage-by-stage tracing with a known-good input localizes the fault to one stage, so you replace only what testing proves bad — and you learn the actual cause. Wholesale recapping that happens to fix it teaches nothing and destroys design intent. The reasoning is the product. (§4, §8)
    Q12What does "the person looking from the outside can see more than the person working on the board" mean as a work procedure?
    Re-inspect your own finished work as if someone else had done it: tug-test every pin, check for bridges, verify against photos. Self-review defeats the tunnel vision of having just reflowed it. (§11)
    Q13Order these for a no-power laptop: (a) diode-mode rails, (b) BIOS Win+B recovery, (c) power-entry walk, (d) microscope intake pass.
    (d) microscope intake → (c) power-entry walk → (a) diode-mode rails/shorts → (b) BIOS recovery — though (b) jumps to first whenever the machine powers but shows no picture, because it is free. Order of cheapest-informativeness, with firmware before hardware as an override. (§4, §6)
    Q14A donor MOSFET's part number matches. What must still happen before it goes in the board?
    Diode-test it out of circuit — donor parts from dead boards carry the faults that killed the donor (or its neighbors' fate). Alex diode-tests every donor part before fitting; the HP Spectre case shows what skipping verification costs. (§11, §7)
    Q15Your repair blows the replacement parts a second time. What are the two professional responses — and the unprofessional one?
    Professional: verify the replacement parts themselves (bad donors exist), then widen the fault bracket — collateral damage or plane damage the first diagnosis missed. Unprofessional: a third unverified attempt. Two strikes → time-box, document, no-fix. (§7, §12)
    Q16State the repair-versus-replacement economics rule that decides which devices are worth commercial repair.
    Diagnose where the method is fast and replacement is expensive: high-value boards whose faults concentrate in a few known priors (pro audio, flagship consoles, industrial controllers). Commodity boards where every hour is invisible in the quote are the trap. Richard's "for profit" frame. (§12)
