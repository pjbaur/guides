1 · Mental model Loudness is perception, measured. Level is voltage, counted. Volume is a knob.

Three words get used as synonyms and none of them are. Level is the height of your audio signal — countable in the file. Loudness is how loud a human perceives that signal — estimable by a model of the ear. Volume is where the listener's playback knob sits — yours to predict, not to control. Everything in this guide exists because platforms stopped trusting level and started measuring loudness.

From about 1990 to 2010, recorded music and ads fought the loudness war. Radio and CD playback had no normalization, so the loudest content won the moment of channel-flipping. Mastering engineers crushed dynamics with limiters to squeeze every last decibel of average level out of a fixed peak ceiling. The result: fatiguing audio and a race to the bottom no single participant could stop.

Streaming platforms and podcast apps ended the arms race by changing the referee. Instead of passing your file through untouched, they measure its integrated loudness and apply gain to hit a house target before playback. Deliver hotter than the target and the platform turns you down — your crushed dynamics buy you nothing. Deliver quieter and most platforms turn you up — your dynamics survive. The competitive incentive to crush audio collapses.

Every tool in this guide — YouLean, dpMeter5, ffmpeg's ebur128 — reports some permutation of four measurements. Learn what question each answers and the readouts stop being noise:

Misconception: "louder files sound better on the platform." On a normalizing platform the opposite can happen. Deliver at −8 LUFS and YouTube turns you down 6 dB — your noise floor and crushed dynamics come along for the ride, while the competitor who delivered at −14 keeps breathing dynamics at the same perceived volume. Loudness is not a competition you can win by pushing; it is a target you land on.

Why you cannot skip this with "I'll just watch the meter"

Watching a loudness meter without knowing what it measures produces confident nonsense: chasing the momentary readout with your hand on the gain, or concluding your episode is "too quiet" from a single short-term glance at a pause between sentences. The meter is a model of average human hearing, averaged over defined windows, with silence excluded by defined gates. When you know the windows and the gates, every number on the screen becomes actionable. That is what the next three sections install.

2 · Prerequisite floor You need ears, arithmetic, and one idea about digital audio.

This guide assumes no audio engineering background. It builds every unit from first principles in §3. There is exactly one idea you should bring with you: audio in a computer is a long list of numbers, each number being the air-pressure deviation at one instant.

Hard prerequisites (all three, or the labs won't land)

  1. Sampled audio. A WAV file stores the waveform as thousands of amplitude numbers per second — 44 100 or 48 000 are the common rates. Bigger number = more pressure at that instant. You never have to touch the individual numbers; you just can't think of the file as "analog."
  2. Channels. Mono is one list; stereo is two — left and right — usually near-identical for spoken word. A meter reads the channels together with defined weights (§4).
  3. Arithmetic with negative numbers. All loudness math is one subtraction: gain needed = target − measured. Both sides are negative; the difference still works. −16 − (−22) = +6.

Soft prerequisites (helpful, not required)

  • Any exposure to a recording app — GarageBand, Audacity, Audio Hijack. You know what a record button and an input level are.
  • Rough familiarity with what a compressor or limiter does. Not needed until §9; the guide defines both before using them.
  • Owning the tools in §7. Not required to read; required to do the labs. YouLean's free edition covers most of them.

3 · Decibels from zero A decibel is a ratio. The suffix is the only thing that tells you a ratio to what.

"It's minus 16" means nothing. −16 dBFS is nearly digital maximum; −16 dBSPL is a whisper in a library; −16 LUFS is the most common podcast delivery target on earth. The number carries the arithmetic; the suffix carries the meaning. Collect the suffixes and every audio conversation you'll ever have decodes.

Why decibels exist at all

Your hearing spans roughly a trillion-fold range of air pressures — from the rustle of a leaf (about 0 dBSPL) to the threshold of pain (about 120 dBSPL). Writing that range as raw numbers is unusable. The decibel compresses it with a logarithm: every ×10 in amplitude becomes +20 dB, so the trillion-fold range (1012) fits into 240 dB. The price of compression: you now think in ratios and differences, not absolute amounts.

The two formulas, stated once so they stop being mysterious:

amplitude dB  =  20 · log₁₀( amplitude / reference )     ← sample height, voltage, pressure
power dB      =  10 · log₁₀( power / reference )           ← watts, energy

Doubling the amplitude is +6.02 dB. Doubling the power is +3.01 dB. You will never evaluate a logarithm by hand in this workflow; you will constantly use three memorized anchors:

ChangeAmplitude factorMemorize as
+6 dB×2one doubling of signal height
−6 dB×½one halving
+20 dB×10one decade — the compressor's "10:1" cousin is unrelated

Every formula in this guide becomes safe the moment suffixes are explicit: gain in dBFS moves loudness in LU one-for-one, but a level of −16 dBFS and a loudness of −16 LUFS are different measurements of different things.

Peak, RMS, and the thing neither measures

Three ways to summarize a waveform, in increasing order of "sounds like listening":

  • Peak — the single largest sample magnitude in a window. Guarantees nothing about perceived loudness: a kick drum alone at −1 dBFS peak can be almost inaudible between hits.
  • RMS — the root-mean-square of the samples, i.e. the energy averaged over a window. Much closer to perception; still wrong in a specific, fixable way: it weights all frequencies equally, and your ear does not (§4's K-weighting is the fix).
  • Loudness (LUFS) — RMS per channel, frequency-weighted like an ear, channel-summed, then aggregated over windows with gates. The complete model. §4 dissects it.

The gain-math drill

One pattern covers every adjustment in this guide:

# how much gain to apply, in dB (or LU — same step size):
gain = target − measured

# worked: file measures −22.2 LUFS integrated, target −16:
gain = (−16) − (−22.2) = +6.2 dB

# worked: same file after a +6.2 dB gain, new true peak:
old TP −10.5 dBTP + 6.2 = −4.3 dBTP   → still under −1.0, safe

# worked: file at −12.5 LUFS, TP −0.9 dBTP, target −16:
gain = −3.5 dB, new TP = −4.4 dBTP   → turning DOWN is always safe

Turning up is where ceilings bite: any gain that would push true peak past your ceiling needs a limiter, not just a fader. That trade-off — the entire craft of §9 — is why "just normalize it" is simultaneously always correct as a goal and never sufficient as an instruction.

Rule of thumb worth memorizing. Perceived "twice as loud" is about +10 LU. A 1 LU error is one just-noticeable difference for trained ears. Platform tolerances of ±1 LU (§6) therefore exist because most listeners cannot hear less.

4 · How a meter thinks BS.1770 in five stages: weight, square, sum, window, gate.

A loudness meter is not a level meter with a nicer label. It runs your signal through a five-stage model of average human hearing, defined — arithmetic and all — in ITU-R BS.1770, with the M/S/I display conventions your tools follow added by the EBU (§5). Every tool you own that says LUFS implements this stack. Learn the five stages once and you can predict what every readout will do before you touch it.

Stage 1–2 · Weight the spectrum like an ear, then square

The ear is not equally sensitive to all frequencies. At conversation level it is roughly 20 dB less sensitive to 60 Hz than to 1 kHz, and a few dB more sensitive around 2–4 kHz. A plain RMS calculation treats them all the same. BS.1770's fix is the K-weighting filter — two stages bolted in front of the energy math:

  • a high-pass around 60 Hz (the "RLB" curve), which attenuates rumble the ear mostly discounts — handling noise, HVAC, plosive thumps;
  • a high shelf lifting about +4 dB above roughly 2 kHz, matching the ear's extra sensitivity where consonants and presence live.

After filtering, each channel's samples are squared — energy, not amplitude — because perceived loudness tracks energy. This is why loudness is a power-domain measurement: the 10·log half of §3's formulas.

Stage 3 · Sum the channels with weights

Energy from all channels adds. Stereo left and right each count fully (weight 1.0); in 5.1 surround, the rear channels get 1.41 (×2 energy) because sounds behind you read as louder. Spoken-word work is stereo or mono, so this stage is invisible to you — but it explains a classic gotcha: the same material in mono measures ~−3 LUFS louder than one channel alone, because two channels of identical energy sum to +3.01 LU.

Stage 4 · Window it

The momentary and short-term windows below are EBU Mode metering definitions (Tech 3341, §5); BS.1770 itself defines the 400 ms gating grid and the integrated result — the windows are how meters display the algorithm's building blocks.

The overlap matters in practice: momentary is deliberately twitchy, short-term deliberately settled. If you stare at momentary during a pause between sentences you will conclude you are failing a target you are actually hitting — the pause is not your program.

LRA · loudness range

Loudness Range compresses the whole program's dynamics into one number: after gating, it takes the short-term loudness of all blocks, and reports the spread between the 10th and 95th percentiles (from EBU Tech 3342, riding on R128). Percentiles make it robust — one scream or one door slam cannot blow it up.

LRA is descriptive, not a delivery spec — no major platform rejects on it — but it is the honest answer to "how compressed is my audio?" and the number to watch when you process speech for car and earbud listeners.

True peak · the ceiling that isn't on the samples

Your file is a list of numbers; the rebuilt analog waveform those numbers represent passes between them. Reconstruction can therefore overshoot the largest sample — an inter-sample peak. Two samples at 0.99 with the true sine crest falling between them rebuild to a wave taller than either:

# full-scale 1 kHz sine, generated and measured locally:
sample peak:  +0.02 dBFS   ← the tallest number in the file
true peak:    +3.0 dBFS    ← the rebuilt wave, 4× oversampled

Nearly 3 dB of overshoot hiding in a file whose samples never touched the ceiling. This is why the delivery world measures dBTP — sample level, reconstructed with 4× oversampling (more for audio between sample points) — and why ceilings are specified in true peak: −1.0 dBTP is the common spoken-word ceiling, with −2 dBTP or more headroom when a lossy codec will re-encode your file (codecs add their own overshoot; §9).

Misconception: "integrated is the average of the momentary readout." Close, but the gates change it — and the direction is not predictable without re-measuring. A file whose quiet 20-minute outro gets gated measures louder integrated than any average-of-everything would suggest. Never derive integrated by hand from the dancing numbers; let the meter run the whole file (§9, §7's offline analysis).

LUFS, LKFS, LU — the naming tangle

LKFS (loudness, K-weighted, relative to full scale) is the name ITU and US law use. LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) is the EBU's name for the identical scale. The numbers are interchangeable: −24 LKFS = −24 LUFS. A LU is a unit of difference on that same scale — you gain 3 LU, you deliver AT −16 LUFS. When a standard quotes tolerance "±0.5 LU," it means the same half-deibel step as "±0.5 dB" applied to loudness.

5 · The standards One algorithm, three rulebooks. ITU wrote the ruler; everyone else wrote the numbers.

ITU-R BS.1770 defines how to measure — the algorithm from §4, nothing about targets. Every document you have ever been told to comply with is a policy layer on top of it: the EBU's R128 for European broadcast, ATSC's A/85 for US television (with actual law behind it), the AES's TD1008 for streaming, and each platform's private §6 policy. Learn the stack once and every "which standard applies?" question dissolves.

ITU-R BS.1770 · the ruler

First published 2006, revised six times, current revision BS.1770-5 (November 2023): "Algorithms to measure audio programme loudness and true-peak audio level." It specifies the K-weighting filter pair, the channel weights, the 400 ms gating block with 75% overlap, both gates (−70 LKFS absolute, then relative at −10 LU), and true peak via 4× oversampling (48 → 192 kHz), reported in dBTP. Revision history worth knowing:

For stereo and 5.1 work — everything in this guide — the revisions are arithmetic-identical: ATSC A/85:2026 states it outright ("The BS.1770-3, -4, and -5 versions all have identical algorithms for Loudness and True-Peak measurement of stereo and 5.1 content"). Any BS.1770-2-or-later meter, including YouLean, dpMeter5 and ffmpeg's ebur128, produces comparable numbers.

EBU R128 + EBU Mode · Europe's rulebook (and your meter's vocabulary)

The European Broadcasting Union wrapped BS.1770 in practice: R128 (current version 5.0, November 2023) sets broadcast delivery at −23.0 LUFS, with tolerance ±1.0 LU where the target isn't practically attainable (live production) and ±0.2 LU for quality-control workflows that account for measurement error. Production true peak shall not exceed −1 dBTP (±0.3 dB measurement tolerance), with lower distribution limits per its Tech 3344 companion. Its history is worth one line: the relative gate moved from −8 LU to −10 LU in 2011, and the 2014 edition's "±0.5 LU" tolerance is superseded — if an old tutorial quotes it, the tutorial is a decade stale.

R128's companion Tech 3341 defines EBU Mode metering — and here is a correction most guides never make: momentary (400 ms) and short-term (3 s) are EBU Mode definitions, not part of BS.1770 itself. BS.1770's 400 ms blocks are the gating grid for integrated loudness; the M/S/I display convention your tools follow comes from the EBU. Tech 3342 defines LRA. Supplements cover the edges: s1 short-form content (ads), s2 streaming (produce to −23; only where devices lack gain may distribution loudness sit −20 to −16 LUFS), s3 radio, s4 cinematic.

ATSC A/85 + CALM Act · the one with legal teeth

US digital television anchors on A/85 (current edition July 2026): −24 LKFS target loudness (anchored to dialogue for long-form content), tolerance about ±2 dB, and true peak below −2 dBTP — the US broadcast ceiling is 1 dB tighter than Europe's, headroom against downstream processing. What makes A/85 unique is the law riding on it: the CALM Act (2010) directed the FCC to stop commercials from being louder than the shows around them, and since December 13, 2012, 47 CFR §73.682(e) has required compliance with A/85 (the 2013 edition is the version incorporated by reference) for commercial ad transmission on US TV. Loudness normalization, enforced by statute, with fines behind it — the end state the loudness war was always heading toward.

AES TD1008 · the streaming recommendation nobody quotes correctly

The Audio Engineering Society's TD1008 (2021; supersedes TD1004 from 2015) is the honest attempt to give streaming and podcasting one set of numbers. Its headline choices: integrated loudness above −20 LUFS always (mobile devices lack gain to lift quieter content), speech and podcast content at −18 LUFS (+2 LU tolerance), track-normalized music at −16, album-loudest-track at −14, and −1 dBTP at the input of lossy codecs — with the explicit warning that codec overshoot grows as bitrate falls. Its most quoted reasoning: speech normalized 2 LU below music (−18 vs −16) because speech reads perceptually louder than music at equal LUFS — the model under-weights something about speech, so the numbers compensate. Where does Apple's −16 sit? At the loud end of the AES speech window — tighter, louder, and in practice the number podcasters actually hit. A mono −19 convention circulates in host documentation (stereo-to-mono sums +3 LU, so −16 stereo ≈ −19 mono playback); it is community convention, not an Apple or AES figure.

Why broadcast sits at −23/−24 while streaming sits at −16/−14. Broadcast chains were built with headroom margins and legacy alignment levels; a −23 LUFS program with LRA to spare plays through a transmission chain that adds processing without crushing. Mobile playback has limited available gain, so streaming needs program loudness closer to playback level — AES's "not below −20" floor exists exactly for the quiet end. Neither number is "more correct"; they are engineering answers to different constraint sets.

How we got here · five dates

  • 1994–2005 · The loudness war escalates with the CD: look-ahead limiters make brutal mastering cheap; Oasis (1995), the loudness poster children, peak around 2005 when the trend arguably crests.
  • 2001 · ReplayGain — David Robinson's open standard for perceived-loudness normalization of digital files. Player-side normalization is born; foobar2000 and friends adopt it; its modern successors measure with BS.1770.
  • 2009–2012 · Spotify ships loudness normalization (on by default from the start; Ian Shepherd titled his October 2009 piece "Spotify Ends The Loudness War"). The CALM Act takes effect in the US, December 13, 2012.
  • 2014–2015 · YouTube's playback normalization arrives (reported March 2015, reference around −13 at the time).
  • 2017–2019 · Spotify drops its reference from −11 to −14 LUFS to match its neighbors; YouTube moves to exactly −14 LUFS (measured 2019). The war is over everywhere that normalizes; the survivors are the platforms that don't (§6, Rumble).
Misconception: "−16 is the podcast standard." −16 LKFS is Apple's delivery spec — one platform's request, not a universal standard. The AES recommends −18 for speech; Spotify would like −14; the EBU would say −23 if you asked about broadcast. The universal master in §6 is a pragmatic compromise across §6's policies, not a standard anyone voted on. Standards define the ruler; you still choose which game to play.

6 · What platforms do Same unit everywhere. Different policy everywhere else.

Every normalizing platform runs the same BS.1770 arithmetic on your file — but they differ in the target, the direction of adjustment, and whether they publish any of it. Three policies cover the field: turn down only (loud content is attenuated, quiet content plays quiet), both directions (quiet content is boosted up to a headroom limit), and preconditioned delivery (you hit the number; the player just reads metadata). Know each platform's policy and you can predict what happens to your file before you upload it.

YouTube · measured, not promised

YouTube's help pages state no LUFS number at all — only that the player makes "automatic adjustments to volume levels and sound mixing." The −14 figure that every tutorial repeats comes from measurement: Ian Shepherd's MeterPlugs read the playback reference off "Stats for nerds" in September 2019 and published the change (YouTube's level had previously floated up to 3 dB different). Treat −14 as a well-corroborated observation of player behavior, subject to change without notice.

The behavioral fact that matters more than the number: YouTube only turns audio down, never up. Deliver at −14 and you play as-is. Deliver at −9 and 5 dB of your hard-won level is discarded at playback — dynamics already crushed, noise floor riding 5 dB hotter than a −14 competitor's. Deliver at −18 and you simply play 4 LU quieter than everyone else. The strategy writes itself: land on −14, keep true peak ≤ −1 dBTP.

Two wrinkles: Stable Volume, a separate dynamic-range compressor on by default in the main app (toggleable per-viewer in player settings; off for YouTube Music and official music videos), and YouTube Music's own −7 LUFS threshold — music louder than −7 is turned down, music between −7 and −14 plays at its mastered level. A dense −9 master plays louder on YouTube Music than on main YouTube. If your content is music-first, that asymmetry rewards keeping some level; if it is speech-first, ignore it and target −14.

Rumble · the honest answer is "unknown"

This guide dug for one and found nothing: no loudness statement in Rumble's support hub, upload FAQs, or creator studio; no reputable independent measurement indexed anywhere. Third-party guides that recommend "−14 LUFS for Rumble" are applying YouTube convention with no Rumble-specific basis.

What to do with a platform that neither publishes a target nor provably normalizes: deliver the level you want heard. A file that lands at −14 LUFS integrated, ≤ −1 dBTP true peak is loud enough to compete, quiet enough to avoid clipping through any transcoding, and identical to what you send YouTube — one master, zero evidence you should do otherwise. If Rumble quietly normalizes later, that master still lands inside every plausible policy. Revisit if you ever measure playback gain there (§10's method works: capture Rumble playback through Audio Hijack, run it past the meter, compare against your uploaded file's numbers).

Spotify · the only one that boosts (with a catch)

Spotify is the best-documented normalizer: their artist support states verbatim, "We adjust tracks to −14 dB LUFS, according to the ITU 1770 standard," applied at playback across formats. Both directions apply — negative gain to loud masters, positive gain to soft ones — but boosts are capped by true-peak headroom: their own example is a −20 LUFS track with −5 dBTP peaks that only gets lifted to −16, because pushing further would breach the ceiling.

Listeners hold three levers: normalization is toggleable, and Premium subscribers choose Loud (−11, with a limiter), Normal (−14) or Quiet (−19). Albums are normalized as a whole to preserve inter-track dynamics; playlists and shuffle normalize per-track. The web player and third-party devices (TVs, speakers) skip normalization entirely.

The Spotify trap for quiet masters. "You can deliver at −20 and Spotify will lift you to −14" is true only with true-peak headroom to spend. A −20 LUFS master with peaks at −1 dBTP has zero headroom and stays at −20. If you master quiet on purpose, master quiet and clean: peaks well below the ceiling, so the platform's boost is actually available. Speech at −16 LUFS with TP at −6 has room to spare everywhere.

Apple Podcasts · the one that tells you exactly what to do

Apple is the only platform in the spoken-word world with a precise, official, enforceable-sounding spec, and it deserves to be quoted almost in full because it doubles as a mastering checklist:

  • overall loudness around −16 dB LKFS, tolerance ±1 dB;
  • true peak not exceeding −1 dBFS, because their encoding "might clip the signal if the recommended true-peak value is not respected";
  • measurement per ITU-R BS.1770-5 — the exact algorithm §4 dissected;
  • embed loudness metadata so playback engines "automatically and precisely" adjust levels (Apple's own players do this via Sound Check; when enabled, tagged content plays "consistently at −16 dB").

Note the mechanism shift: Apple does not promise blanket server-side gain. You precondition the file; the player reads the tag. Compliance is cheap — §9's workflow produces it as a side effect — and non-compliance is silent: nothing rejects, listeners' apps just disagree about you.

SoundCloud, and the rest

SoundCloud does normalize — their own help says so — which contradicts a decade of stale forum lore that it plays files untouched. Their mastering guidance recommends delivering −14 LUFS integrated with at least −1 dBTP headroom (below −2 dBTP for loud masters). Tidal, Amazon Music and Deezer normalize with turn-down-only behavior per community modeling, targets around −14 but officially unverified; Bandcamp plays what you upload; Facebook/Instagram publish nothing, and their 2023 audio work was encoding, not loudness. Netflix's −27 LKFS dialogue-gated spec is a delivery contract for content sold to Netflix — included here because it shows up in every "loudness targets" list and is otherwise irrelevant to podcasters.

So what do you deliver?

The wrong question: "which platform's number do I optimize for?" The right one: "what single file lands acceptably everywhere I publish?" For spoken word — podcasts, voiceover, commentary video:

The universal spoken-word master. −16 LUFS integrated, true peak ≤ −1.5 dBTP. Hits Apple's spec dead center; plays un-boosted but competitive on YouTube (−2 LU under its threshold — per­ceptibly one notch quieter than −14 targets, a fair trade for one master everywhere); lifts cleanly on Spotify, which has headroom to spare; sane on Rumble and everywhere unverified. If YouTube is demonstrably your dominant platform and speech is your content, a −14 LUFS variant is the second master worth keeping.

For music-led content the calculus changes: YouTube Music's −7 threshold rewards density, Spotify caps boosts, Apple Music sits near −14 with its own Sound Check behavior. The honest summary for musicians: deliver at −14 LUFS with the dynamics you can defend, or master to taste knowing turn-down-only platforms will punish nobody but your dynamic range. 9 · Post-production Measure, subtract, apply, re-measure. The re-measure is the workflow.

Hitting a loudness target is arithmetic you can do in your head — the target minus the measurement is the gain — wrapped in one trap: pushing level up pushes peaks toward the ceiling, and past the ceiling the arithmetic needs a limiter, not a fader. The five-step loop below runs the arithmetic, respects the ceiling, and ends by measuring the result instead of predicting it. Every number in it is real, from the 40-second lab file built in Lab 1.

Step 1 · Measure the finished edit

Two roads, same answer: YouLean PRO's drag-and-drop (drop the file on the plugin's window, read I / TP / LRA), or ffmpeg's ebur128 scanner, which prints the identical BS.1770 numbers and costs nothing:

ffmpeg -hide_banner -nostats -loglevel info -i pink-dyn.wav \
  -af ebur128=peak=true -f null -
(per-frame readouts scroll by; the end-of-file Summary is what you want)

  Integrated loudness:
    I:         -22.2 LUFS
    Threshold: -33.9 LUFS
  Loudness range:
    LRA:        14.0 LU
  True peak:
    Peak:      -10.5 dBFS

Expect: I about −22 LUFS for this lab file (loud/medium/quiet/silent stretch noise); LRA 14; TP −10.5. YouLean shows the same I within ~0.1 LU — two BS.1770 implementations agreeing is the sanity check that both are healthy.

Step 2 · Compute the move

target  −16 LUFS   (§6 universal spoken-word master)
measured −22.2 LUFS
gain    = (−16) − (−22.2) = +6.2 dB

predicted true peak = −10.5 + 6.2 = −4.3 dBTP   → ceiling −1.5 not breached → plain gain is enough

The prediction is the decision point for the whole workflow: if predicted TP breaches your ceiling, a pure gain move will clip — that is the case that needs a limiter (Audio Hijack's Volume block with look-ahead limiter, or FinalLoud3 doing the whole job). Here, +6.2 dB leaves the peaks at −4.3 dBTP with room to spare, so a plain gain stage is honest work.

Step 3 · Apply it — and meet the one-pass trap

ffmpeg's loudnorm filter is the free reference implementation of "hit the target," and its default behavior teaches a lesson worth the detour. One-pass mode uses a dynamic normalizer that compresses as it goes:

ffmpeg -i pink-dyn.wav -af loudnorm=I=-16:TP=-1.5:LRA=7 \
  -ar 48000 pink-16-onepass.wav

Input Integrated:    -22.3 LUFS      Input True Peak:     -10.5 dBTP
Output Integrated:   -18.3 LUFS      Output True Peak:     -5.8 dBTP
Output LRA:           8.4 LU  ← asked for -16; got -18.3, and LRA crushed 14.1 → 8.4

It missed the target by 2.3 LU and squeezed the loudness range nearly in half — because one-pass loudnorm is riding gain dynamically and compressing to satisfy its LRA parameter. For finished programs that is vandalism. The fix is two-pass linear: pass one only measures; you feed those measurements back, and pass two applies one fixed gain with no dynamics touched:

# pass 1: measure only (loudnorm prints the numbers, writes nothing you keep)
ffmpeg -i pink-dyn.wav -af loudnorm=I=-16:TP=-1.5:LRA=15:print_format=summary -f null -
   Input Integrated: -22.3 LUFS · Input True Peak: -10.5 dBTP · Input LRA: 14.1 LU · Threshold: -33.9 LUFS

# pass 2: linear=true + those measured values = one fixed gain, no compression
ffmpeg -i pink-dyn.wav -af loudnorm=I=-16:TP=-1.5:LRA=15:\
measured_I=-22.3:measured_TP=-10.5:measured_LRA=14.1:measured_thresh=-33.9:linear=true \
  -ar 48000 pink16.wav

In the GUI world, dpMeter5's loudness match is this same move — point it at −16 and it applies the corrective gain — and FinalLoud3 wraps measure-apply-limit into one block. Understand the two-pass pattern and every tool's behavior, including when it is quietly compressing instead of shifting, becomes legible.

Step 4 · Re-measure — never skip

ffmpeg -hide_banner -nostats -loglevel info -i pink16.wav -af ebur128=peak=true -f null -

  Integrated loudness:
    I:         -15.9 LUFS     ← target −16, landed 0.1 over: inside every tolerance in §6
  Loudness range:
    LRA:        14.0 LU        ← dynamics untouched, unlike the one-pass attempt
  True peak:
    Peak:       -4.2 dBFS      ← −10.5 + 6.3 ≈ prediction honored

Why re-measure when the math said +6.2? Because of §4's gates: gain can change which blocks the relative gate admits, monophonic downmixes shift the sum, and tool rounding is real. The loop's last step is always an instrument reading, not an inference. YouLean PRO's drag-and-drop makes it a five-second habit; ffmpeg makes it a scriptable one.

  • Metadata: Apple Podcasts asks for loudness metadata to be embedded so players can auto-adjust — podcast encoders (including Apple's own Podcasts bundle tooling and most hosts' upload pipelines) handle this from your measured values; the number they tag is only as good as the measurement you feed them.
  • Name the numbers: a filename suffix or a note (ep042_I-15.9_TP-4.2) makes month-later "why does this one sound right?" a lookup instead of a re-measure.
Misconception: "a normalizer is a normalizer." Tools with the same goal apply wildly different processing: two-pass linear shifts one fixed gain (dynamics intact); one-pass dynamic rides gain continuously (dynamics altered); a true-peak limiter trades transient peaks for level; a compressor shapes range by design. §6 platforms do the linear kind. Match their behavior in production and what you measured is what listeners get.

11 · Voiceover craft The listener sets volume once. Consistency is the whole job.

A listener touches the volume knob at second one and never again. Every inconsistency after that — a hot paragraph, a fading guest, a floor that rises between sentences — is experienced as a defect. Loudness targets (§6) and measurement (§4) are how you make consistency verifiable instead of vibes; this section is the craft that makes it true at the source, before any plugin helps.

Capture: the three wins at the mic

  1. Fixed distance, 15–20 cm, slightly off-axis. Cardioid mics boost bass as you close in (proximity effect), so distance swings become EQ swings no downstream tool will fully undo. Off-axis by a hand's width turns plosive puffs of air into missable puffs instead of thumps.
  2. Gain at the interface, not later. Set input so sustained speech reads −18 to −12 dBFS and excited peaks approach −6. Signal acquired this far above the noise floor makes every later stage cleaner — digital gain amplifies the floor with the voice (§8's warning, now with a reason).
  3. Tame the room before the computer. Blankets, closet, carpet — any soft surface within a meter of the mic. A quiet, dead room at −55 dBFS floor beats any denoise plugin: denoise trades artifacts for hiss, and §8's ML Speech Denoise is a rescue tool, not a substitute.

Consistency drills (measurement-guided practice)

  • Take matching: record the same 30-second script twice, an hour apart. Measure both short-term profiles (YouLean graph or §10's live rig). Matching within ±1 LU across the take is the skill; distance drift and energy drift show up here first.
  • Paragraph steering: read a page aloud watching short-term only. Practice keeping S inside a 3 LU band without changing vocal effort — tiny input-gain anticipation on deep breaths is the pro move this drill builds.
  • Reference read: keep one mastered clip you admire (§10 audited, numbers known) and one of your own best takes. A/B them before sessions; your target sound now has coordinates.

14 · Capstone One two-minute voiceover, produced end to end against §6's universal master.

Produce, land, verify, and defend a single spoken-word deliverable at −16 LUFS integrated, ≤ −1.5 dBTP, LRA 5–8 LU — the numbers every platform in §6 accepts. The capstone exercises the full spine: live capture discipline (§8), measurement (§4, §9), gain work (§9), and audit-grade verification (§10). Budget 45 minutes.

The deliverable

  • Script: any 90–120 second read — a product blurb, channel intro, or a paragraph of this guide read aloud.
  • Master: WAV, 48 kHz, integrated −16 LUFS ±0.5, true peak ≤ −1.5 dBTP, LRA 5–8 LU.
  • Delivery copy: AAC encode of the master (the format most platforms re-encode anyway).
  • Report: your four numbers, the tool that produced each, and one screenshot or terminal block as evidence.

Phases

  1. Capture (§8, §11). Build the Lab 4 chain. Set input gain so sustained speech peaks −18 to −12 dBFS. Record the read plus 10 s of room tone. Ride short-term into −20 to −15 LUFS while speaking.
  2. Edit. Trim mistakes; leave natural pauses (gates will ignore dead air — Lab 2 proved it). Light processing only, in §11's order. Do not chase loudness here.
  3. Measure (§9 step 1). YouLean drag-and-drop or ebur128: record I, TP, LRA, threshold.
  4. Compute (§9 step 2). gain = −16 − I. Predicted TP = TP + gain. Decide: plain gain, or gain-into-limiter if the prediction breaches −1.5 dBTP.
  5. Apply (§9 step 3). dpMeter5 match in session, or two-pass loudnorm at the terminal, or FinalLoud3 if you own it. Linear only — Lab 3's lesson.
  6. Verify (§9 step 4). Re-measure the result. Not within ±0.5 LU of −16 or under the ceiling? Loop back to step 5 — never "close enough."
  7. Deliver (§9 step 5). Encode the AAC copy, name the numbers (vo01_I-15.8_TP-2.1.m4a), and file the report next to the WAV master.
  8. Audit (stretch goal). Upload anywhere with a player you can measure, then run §10's capture or Lab 5's overlay check. Observed playback behavior beats every table in §6, including this guide's.

Q1A platform that only turns audio down receives your −10 LUFS master. What does the listener hear, and what did you lose?
They hear −14-ish perceived loudness like everyone else — your master is attenuated 4 dB at playback. What you lost: dynamics crushed in mastering and a noise floor riding 4 dB hotter than necessary. (§1)
Q2−16 dBFS, −16 dBSPL, −16 LUFS: same number, three different facts. Name each fact.
−16 dBFS: 16 dB under digital full scale (a level). −16 dBSPL: a whisper-quiet room (air pressure vs 20 µPa). −16 LUFS: the podcast delivery loudness target (BS.1770 perception model). (§3)
Q3Your file measures −22.2 LUFS. Target −16. What gain, and what must you check before applying it?
Gain = −16 − (−22.2) = +6.2 dB. Check predicted true peak: old TP + 6.2 — if it breaches your ceiling (−1 to −2 dBTP), the gain must go through a limiter, not a plain fader. (§9)
Q4What are the two gates in integrated loudness, and what does each exclude?
Absolute gate at −70 LUFS excludes hiss/room tone. Relative gate at (ungated loudness − 10 LU) excludes blocks far below the program — long quiet passages and pauses. Applied to overlapping 400 ms blocks. (§4)
Q5Why can the true peak of a file exceed 0 dBFS when no sample does?
Reconstruction: the rebuilt analog waveform passes between samples, so the crest can top the tallest sample. Measuring it requires oversampling (4×). Hence dBTP and the −1/−2 dBTP delivery ceilings. (§4)
Q6Same content duplicated from one channel into two (mono → stereo). What happens to the loudness reading?
Rises by 3.01 LU — channel energies sum (weight 1.0 each). Classic gotcha when comparing mono and stereo versions of a program. (§4)
Q7Which readout do you steer while recording speech live, and into what box?
Short-term (3 s window), into −20 to −15 LUFS for a −16-ish program; momentary is the spike alarm, integrated is meaningless mid-flight. (§8)
Q8Where does the meter go in an Audio Hijack chain, and why exactly there?
Last, after every gain/limit block and before the recorder — it must observe exactly what gets recorded. A meter upstream of gain flatters you. (§8)
Q9One-pass loudnorm asked for −16 and delivered −18.3 with LRA 8.4 (input 14.1). What happened, and what's the fix?
Dynamic mode rode gain continuously and compressed to satisfy its LRA setting — missed target, crushed dynamics. Fix: two-pass linear with the measured values (lands −15.9, LRA preserved). (§9, Lab 3)
Q10Name the only major platform that boosts quiet content, and the catch.
Spotify (−14, both directions, Loud −11/Quiet −19 user-selectable). The catch: boosts are capped by true-peak headroom — a quiet master with peaks near the ceiling stays quiet. (§6)
Q11What is YouTube's official loudness target?
Trick question: none is published. −14 LUFS is a third-party measurement (Shepherd/MeterPlugs, 2019, corroborated 2026) of player behavior — turn-down only. (§6)
Q12What does Apple Podcasts ask for, in which unit spelling, and by what mechanism is it honored at playback?
Around −16 dB LKFS ±1 dB, true peak ≤ −1 dBFS, per BS.1770; honored via embedded loudness metadata that playback engines (Sound Check) use to adjust levels. (§6)
Q13Rumble: what loudness behavior is documented, and what do you deliver?
Nothing documented — no target, no normalization statement, no credible measurement. Deliver the universal master (−16 LUFS, ≤ −1.5 dBTP); verify empirically per §10 if it matters. (§6)
Q14Which of your tools changes audio, and which only watches?
Watch only: YouLean (analysis-only, always safe in chain). Watch + gain on command: dpMeter5 (loudness match). Gain + limit automatically: FinalLoud3. Watch level only: Audio Hijack's Peak/RMS blocks. (§7)
Q15You append 20 seconds of silence to a file. What happens to its integrated loudness?
Nothing — the absolute gate excludes it. Measured proof: the lab file read −22.2 LUFS before and after. Same reason trailing outros don't drag podcast numbers down. (§4, Lab 2)
Q16Why deliver −2 dBTP instead of −1 for platform uploads?
Platforms re-encode to lossy formats; codecs reconstruct slightly different waveforms and add their own overshoot. The extra headroom absorbs it so playback doesn't clip. (§9)
