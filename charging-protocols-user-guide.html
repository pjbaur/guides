1 · Mental model Charging is a conversation before it is a current.

A charger never pushes power into your phone. It offers power, and the phone decides how much to take. Every protocol in this guide, from a 2007 resistor to a 2026 PD packet, is a different answer to one question: how does the phone learn what the charger can safely deliver, and how does it ask for more?

Three parties take part in every wired charge. The source (charger) knows its limits. The sink (phone, laptop) knows what its battery wants right now. The cable sits between them and has limits of its own. Old protocols ignored the cable. Modern ones interrogate it.

The protocols differ mainly in where the conversation happens. There are only four channels, and once you know them, every acronym in this guide slots into one:

The three laws of charging

  1. The default is always safe and slow. Every protocol starts at 5 V with a small current. Higher power exists only after both ends prove they understand each other. Silence means 5 V.
  2. The weakest link sets the power. Charger limit, cable limit, phone limit, and phone temperature: the delivered watts are the minimum of all four, not the charger's label.
  3. Power = voltage × current, and heat = current² × resistance. Every fast-charge scheme is a strategy for getting more watts without cooking a cable connector or the phone. Raise voltage (QC, PD fixed), or raise current and move the conversion (VOOC, PPS direct charging). See §4.
Misconception: "a 100 W charger will push 100 W into my phone." The label is the menu, not the meal. A 100 W charger connected to a phone that asks for 9 V × 2.22 A delivers 20 W. The charger cannot force more, and that is the whole safety model.

2 · Prerequisite floor Four ideas. If these are solid, the rest is vocabulary.

Charging protocols are electrical engineering wearing a software costume. You do not need to design a buck converter, but you do need four ideas to read a USB tester without guessing.

  • P = V × I. 9 V × 2 A = 18 W. 20 V × 5 A = 100 W. Every PD offer is a (voltage, current) pair; the watts are derived.
  • Loss in a wire = I² × R. Double the current, quadruple the heat in the cable and connector. That is why 5 A needs a thicker, certified cable and 3 A does not.
  • Lithium-ion charges in two phases. Constant current (CC) until the cell nears about 4.2–4.5 V, then constant voltage (CV) with current tapering off. Fast charging only matters in the CC phase, which is why "0 to 50%" marketing numbers look so much better than "0 to 100%".
  • A converter trades voltage for current. A buck converter turning 9 V into 4.2 V roughly doubles current, minus losses that become heat inside whatever box the converter lives in.
Self-check: can you answer these in your head? 2 minutes
  1. A charger offers 15 V at 3 A. How many watts? (45 W)
  2. A cable runs 3 A with 0.2 Ω round-trip resistance. How much heat, and what voltage drop? (1.8 W; 0.6 V)
  3. Same cable at 5 A? (5 W; 1 V. That is why the 5 A spec insists on an e-marked cable.)
  4. Your phone shows "charging slowly" at 85%. Is the charger broken? (No. That is the CV phase.)

Missed two or more? Re-read the bullets above before §5. Everything later assumes them.

4 · Where the heat goes Every fast-charge design is a decision about which box gets warm.

Watts are easy. Cool watts are hard. A charger can make 240 W all day; a 9 mm-thick phone cannot shed more than about one watt of waste heat without the battery suffering. The entire proprietary-protocol war of 2014–2022 was a fight over where to put the voltage conversion.

TI's numbers make the argument concrete. A good buck charger in the phone is over 90% efficient at 6 A, and that still means more than 2 W of heat inside the handset. A 2:1 switched-capacitor charger reaches about 97% at 6 A into the battery with only 3 A in the cable, and loses under 800 mW. The catch: a charge pump does not regulate. It just halves whatever arrives. So the charger has to hit the target voltage and current exactly, which a fixed 9 V PDO cannot do. You need a programmable source.

The two-cell trick

Put two cells in series (a "2S" pack) and the battery sits at 8–9 V instead of 4 V. Now the phone can take 20 V from the cable and divide it to about 10 V at double the current inside the phone. SuperVOOC 240W and Quick Charge 5 both do this. The display still shows one battery percentage; the pack is two cells wired as one.

Why this matters for you When a Samsung or Pixel refuses to "fast charge" from a perfectly good 65 W laptop charger, the reason is almost always that the phone wants PPS for its charge pump and the charger only offers fixed voltages. Fixed-voltage fallback runs through the buck path, which the phone throttles to stay cool.

5 · Legacy USB & BC 1.2 Before packets, there were resistors and a 0.6 V probe.

The original USB spec did not think of phones as batteries. It thought of them as mice. A device got 100 mA until the computer said it could have 500 mA, and a wall charger, having no computer inside, had no way to say anything at all. Battery Charging 1.2 is the patch: a tiny analog handshake on the data lines that lets a phone recognize "this port is just for power".

6 · Type-C Current One resistor on one pin replaced the whole BC 1.2 dance.

USB-C added two pins, CC1 and CC2, whose first job is analog: a resistor in the charger and a resistor in the phone form a voltage divider, and the voltage on that pin tells the phone how much current it may draw at 5 V. No packets, no firmware. Up to 15 W from a resistor.

Below 0.276 V the sink treats CC as disconnected. The gaps between bands (0.613–0.745 V and 1.165–1.368 V) are guard zones. The source must debounce a new attach for tCCDebounce = 100–200 ms and turn VBUS on within tVBUSON ≤ 275 ms.

The 56 kΩ legacy-cable rule A USB-A-to-C cable has no CC wire back to the charger, so the resistor lives inside the C plug. It must be 56 kΩ. Cheap early cables used 10 kΩ, telling the phone "3 A available" when the USB-A port behind it might only manage 500 mA. That is how the 2015-era Benson Leung cable reviews started. If a phone pulls suspicious current from a laptop USB-A port, check the cable.
Misconception: "0.66 V and 1.23 V are the thresholds." Those numbers circulate widely, but they are not in Type-C R2.5. The current tables give ranges, listed above. Design to the ranges.

7 · USB PD, 1.0 → 3.0 The charger publishes a menu. The phone orders one line. Both sign.

USB Power Delivery turns charging into a tiny network protocol. Once the resistors in §6 establish attachment, the charger sends a Source_Capabilities message listing every voltage and current it offers. The phone picks exactly one with a Request. The charger says Accept, moves the voltage, and says PS_RDY. Four messages, under half a second, and you are at 20 V.

PD 1.0: the version that did not happen

PD 1.0 (5 July 2012) predates USB-C. It signalled by superimposing 24 MHz BFSK on VBUS itself, and defined five fixed profiles:

ProfileOfferMax
15 V × 2 A10 W
25 V × 2 A, 12 V × 1.5 A18 W
3+ 12 V × 3 A36 W
4+ 20 V × 3 A60 W
5+ 20 V × 5 A100 W

Profiles were deprecated in PD 2.0 v1.2 (March 2016) and replaced by Power Rules. BFSK was deprecated in PD 3.0. Treat PD 1.0 as history: you will not meet it in the wild.

PD 2.0: BMC on CC

PD 2.0 (11 August 2014) moved signalling onto the CC wire as Biphase Mark Coding at about 300 kbps (270–330 kbps allowed), with 4b5b symbol encoding. It also introduced addressing: SOP packets go to the port partner, SOP′ and SOP″ go to the chips in the near and far cable plugs.

8 · Programmable Power Supply (PPS) Stop picking from a menu. Dial the exact voltage.

Fixed PDOs force the phone to accept 9 V and throw the difference away as heat. PPS lets the phone say "give me 8.62 V at 2.9 A" and then keep adjusting it every few seconds as the battery fills. The charger becomes the phone's external, remote-controlled charging circuit. This is what Samsung Super Fast Charging, Google Pixel, and Qualcomm QC 4+/5 actually run on.

9 · EPR, AVS & PD 3.2 Past 20 V, the protocol stops trusting anyone.

PD 3.1 (May 2021) raised the ceiling from 100 W to 240 W by adding 28, 36 and 48 V. Everything under 20 V was renamed SPR (Standard Power Range) and the new territory is EPR (Extended Power Range). Because 48 V into a cable rated for 20 V is a genuine hazard, EPR adds a formal entry ceremony, cable interrogation, and a heartbeat.

EPR fixed× 5 ATypical use
28 V140 WApple 140 W, 16" laptops
36 V180 WGaming laptops, monitors
48 V240 WWorkstations, power tools, docks

Entering EPR mode

  1. Normal SPR contract first. The source's 5 V PDO carries an EPR Capable bit. The sink sets EPR Capable in its Request.
  2. Sink sends EPR_Mode (Enter). Source replies EPR_Mode (Enter Acknowledged).
  3. Cable check. The source becomes VCONN source if needed and reads the e-marker with Discover Identity. The cable must report 50 V, 5 A, and EPR Capable. Anything else: Enter Failed.
  4. EPR_Mode (Enter Succeeded), all within tEnterEPR 450–550 ms.
  5. EPR_Source_Capabilities (extended message): positions 1–7 are SPR PDOs, EPR PDOs start at position 8, up to four of them.
  6. EPR_Request → Accept → PS_RDY. The voltage transition may take up to 830–1020 ms.
  7. Heartbeat. The sink sends EPR_KeepAlive every 250–500 ms. Silence past 750–1000 ms and the source drops out of EPR.

Adjustable Voltage Supply (AVS)

AVS is PPS's big sibling, with coarser steps and no current-limit mode. It exists so laptops and tools can ask for, say, 42.3 V without the charger having to build an exotic fixed rail.

10 · Cables & e-markers The cable is the only party that cannot lie about its copper, so it carries a chip that can.

11 · Apple & Samsung dividers Two resistors, one secret handshake.

Before BC 1.2 was widespread, Apple wanted iPads to charge at 2 A+ from its own bricks and not from random ones. The solution was a pair of resistor dividers holding D+ and D− at specific voltages. The phone reads the voltages and knows the current budget. No negotiation, no reply. It is a label written in volts.

Tolerances from TI's TPS2514A: the 2.7 V level is 2.57–2.84 V, the 2.0 V level is 1.9–2.1 V, and the 1.2 V level is 1.12–1.28 V. Smart USB-A chargers use chips like the TPS2514A to cycle through these modes until the attached device starts drawing current.

Why your old iPad brick charges Android phones slowly A phone that only knows BC 1.2 looks for a short between D+ and D−. An Apple divider is not a short, so the phone does not recognize a DCP and may fall back to 500 mA. Many Android vendors added divider detection later. Behavior varies by phone.

12 · Qualcomm Quick Charge Hijack the data lines, raise the voltage, never tell USB-IF.

Quick Charge was the first protocol to reach tens of millions of phones, because it lived where the phones already were: in the Snapdragon power-management chip. QC 2.0 handshake, step by step

  1. Pretend to be a DCP. The charger shorts D+ and D−. The phone runs BC 1.2 and sees a DCP.
  2. Phone holds D+ at 0.325–2 V for ≥ 1.25 s. Its own 0.6 V probe, held long. The charger notices and opens the short.Watch: D− drops, because the short is gone.
  3. Phone pulls D− low for ≥ 1 ms. QC mode entered. VBUS still 5 V.
  4. Phone sets the code. The charger reads the pair and moves VBUS.

14 · UFCS & the law When the market will not converge, somebody makes it.

15 · Qi, Qi2 & MagSafe No wires, so the conversation rides on the power itself.

A wireless charger is a transformer with its core cut in half: one coil in the pad, one in the phone, and a few millimetres of air and glass between them. With no data wires, the phone talks by rhythmically changing how much power it absorbs, and the pad talks back by nudging its frequency. The magnet ring of Qi2 exists for one reason: a misaligned coil wastes energy as heat.

The standards war, briefly

Qi (WPC) won

Inductive, 87–205 kHz

WPC founded 2008, Qi 1.0 in 2010. BPP ≤ 5 W, EPP ≤ 15 W (v1.2, 2015), authentication in v1.3 (2021).

PMA / Powermat merged

Inductive

Founded 2012 by P&G and Powermat; Starbucks tables in 2014. Merged into AirFuel in 2015.

A4WP / Rezence merged

Resonant, 6.78 MHz

Loosely coupled, multi-device. Merged with PMA in 2015 as the AirFuel Alliance. AirFuel RF (released 2023) targets centimetres to metres of range.

19 · Labs Reading is not knowing. A USB tester turns every claim in this guide into a number you can see.

These labs need a USB-C power meter that can read PD messages, like the FNIRSI FNB58 or ChargerLAB POWER-Z KM003C. The companion USB tester shootout compares them. The labs were designed from the specs; "Expect" lines state what the spec predicts, not captured output, so your tester's wording will differ.

Trigger safety Tester trigger modes raise VBUS to 9, 12, 20 V or more. Only trigger with nothing connected on the tester's output, or with an electronic load. A 5 V-only gadget on a manually triggered 20 V line is a dead gadget.
Lab 1 · Read a charger's menu
10 minutes · any USB-C PD charger · tester with PD capture
  1. Plug the tester into the charger, then a phone or laptop into the tester.
  2. Open the tester's PD analyzer or "PDO list" screen.Expect: a list starting with Fixed 5 V, then higher fixed voltages, then PPS lines if supported. At most 7 entries in SPR.
  3. Find the RDO line: which object number the device requested.Expect: the object number and requested current. The live voltage matches that PDO.
  4. Compute the menu's max watts and compare with the label.
Checkpoint: you can name every PDO, whether PPS is present, and which one your device chose.
Lab 2 · Catch the cable veto
10 minutes · a 65 W+ charger · one plain 3 A cable and one 5 A e-marked cable
  1. Repeat Lab 1 with the 3 A cable. Note the current on the 20 V PDO.Expect: 3 A or less on every PDO.
  2. Swap to the 5 A cable. If your tester has an e-marker reader, read the cable VDO first.Expect: e-marker reports 5 A; a 100 W+ charger now lists 5 A on 20 V.
Checkpoint: you have seen the source rewrite its own menu based on the cable (§10).
Lab 3 · Speak Quick Charge by hand
10 minutes · a USB-A QC 3.0 charger · tester with QC trigger · nothing on the output
  1. Plug the tester into the QC charger, nothing downstream. Read D+ and D− voltages if your tester shows them.Expect: VBUS about 5 V. D+ and D− read similar because the charger shorts them (DCP).
  2. Trigger QC 2.0 at 9 V.Expect: VBUS jumps to about 9 V after the ≥ 1.25 s entry hold.
  3. Switch to QC 3.0 and step up and down.Expect: VBUS moves in 0.2 V increments per pulse.
  4. Unplug to reset. The charger falls back to 5 V.
Checkpoint: you have seen that QC is just voltage codes on D+/D− (§12).
Lab 4 · Watch PPS breathe
20 minutes · a PPS charger · a Samsung or Pixel phone below 50%
  1. Charge through the tester and record voltage once a minute for ten minutes.Expect: an odd voltage like 8.3–9.5 V that drifts in small steps, not a flat 9.00 V.
  2. Swap to a fixed-only PD charger of the same wattage.Expect: a flat 9 V and fewer watts.
Checkpoint: you can explain why the phone keeps re-requesting (§8, §4).
Lab 5 · Measure the wireless tax
20 minutes · a Qi or Qi2 pad · tester between pad and its charger
  1. Put the phone centered on the pad. Record input watts after two minutes.
  2. Offset the phone about 1 cm and record again.Expect: the pad draws similar or more power while the phone's own charging rate falls, or charging stops.
  3. Compare with the same phone wired at the same battery level.
Checkpoint: you have a personal number for the wireless tax (§15).

20 · Capstone Audit the drawer.

Everybody owns a drawer of chargers and cables that "all work". The capstone turns that drawer into a known inventory, so you never again wonder why something charges slowly.

Capstone · The charging inventory
60–90 minutes · every charger and cable you own · tester · spreadsheet or paper
  1. Chargers. For each: port type, protocols the tester detects (BC 1.2, QC, PD, PPS, EPR), full PDO list.
  2. Cables. For each C-to-C: e-marker yes/no, rated current, rated voltage (20 V or 50 V), EPR bit. Label them with tape: "3A", "5A", "240".
  3. Devices. For each: charge through the tester from the best charger, record steady-state watts at 20–40% battery, and the protocol used.
  4. Predict, then test. Pick three odd combinations. Predict the result using the simulator logic, then measure.
  5. Retire. Anything with a 10 kΩ A-to-C cable, an unreadable 5 A claim, or no protocol but 5 V × 1 A goes in the e-waste box.
Done when: every device in your house has a named "best charger + cable" pair and you predicted at least two of three odd combinations correctly.

25 · Quiz Eighteen flashcards, self-scored. Aim for 15/18.

Score: 0 learned · 0 review · 0/18 answered
Q1A 100 W charger is connected to a phone. What decides how many watts actually flow?
The minimum of charger limit, cable limit, and what the phone requests (plus the phone's temperature throttling). The charger only offers; the sink orders. (§1)
Q2Name the four channels charging protocols signal on, with one protocol each.
D+/D− (BC 1.2, QC 2/3, VOOC, UFCS) · CC (Type-C Current, USB PD) · VBUS (PD 1.0 BFSK, MediaTek Pump Express) · the magnetic field (Qi). (§1)
Q3How does a BC 1.2 phone recognize a dedicated wall charger?
It drives 0.5–0.7 V on D+ and reads D−. A DCP's D+/D− short (≤ 200 Ω) loops the voltage back above the 0.25–0.4 V reference. (§5)
Q4What Rp values advertise default, 1.5 A and 3 A on a 5 V pull-up, and what is Rd?
56 kΩ, 22 kΩ, 10 kΩ; Rd is 5.1 kΩ in the sink. (§6)
Q5Why must a USB-A-to-C cable contain a 56 kΩ resistor, and what goes wrong with 10 kΩ?
The A side has no CC, so the C plug supplies Rp. 56 kΩ advertises default USB power; 10 kΩ falsely advertises 3 A and a phone may overload the A port. (§6)
Q6List the four messages of a basic PD contract, in order.
Source_Capabilities → Request → Accept → PS_RDY, each acknowledged by GoodCRC. (§7)
Q7Why can a normal Source_Capabilities message list at most seven PDOs?
The header's data-object count field is 3 bits. EPR uses an extended message to list more. (§7)
Q8What maximum power does PD allow with a non-e-marked cable, and why?
60 W (20 V × 3 A). Without an e-marker the source must assume the cable is only rated for 3 A. (§7, §10)
Q9What does PPS let the phone do that fixed PDOs do not?
Request exact voltages in 20 mV steps and currents in 50 mA steps, re-requested at least every 10 s, with the source current-limiting. This lets the phone use an efficient charge pump. (§8)
Q10Why does direct charging keep the phone cooler?
The lossy regulation happens in the charger. The phone only halves voltage with a ~97% charge pump instead of a ~90% buck converter. (§4)
Q11Name the three EPR fixed voltages and the maximum power.
28, 36, 48 V at 5 A; up to 240 W. (§9)
Q12What must the cable report for EPR entry to succeed?
50 V rating, 5 A, and the EPR Capable bit in its Cable VDO. (§9, §10)
Q13How does QC 2.0 select 9 V?
After entry (D+ held ~0.6 V ≥ 1.25 s, then D− pulled low), the phone sets D+ = 3.3 V and D− = 0.6 V. (§12)
Q14Is Quick Charge 5 a competitor to USB PD?
No. QC 4 onward is built on USB PD and PPS; QC 5 adds Qualcomm's certification and 2S charge-pump hardware. (§12)
Q15Why does a SuperVOOC brick with a generic cable charge slowly?
SuperVOOC needs the vendor cable's signal pin or ID chip and its high-current rating. Without it, the brick falls back to a basic rate. (§13)
Q16What does the EU common-charger directive require of a phone charging above 15 W?
A USB-C receptacle and USB PD support; proprietary modes may exist alongside. Phones since 28 Dec 2024, laptops since 28 Apr 2026. (§14)
Q17How does a Qi phone talk to the pad, and how does the pad talk back?
Phone → pad by ASK load modulation at 2 kbps; pad → phone by FSK on the carrier at about 200 bps. (§15)
Q18Is there a USB PD 4.0 or a Qi 50 W standard as of September 2026?
No. The current PD spec is Revision 3.2 Version 1.2; Qi 50 W was only discussed at a June 2026 WPC meeting, with no published spec. (§16)
